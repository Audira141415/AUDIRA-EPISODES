Not an unexpected opinion to be published in The Chicago Daily Defender, a 97-year-old, tradition-rich African-American newspaper. Unexpected, however, was the writer, Joe Ruklick, 63, the only white member of the newspaper's 22-person editorial staff.

Ruklick is unusual in another aspect. He may be the only former big-time pro athlete in print journalism. The journalistic forms favored by ex-jocks are radio and television.

Ruklick was a kind of newsmaker himself. It's an old story, but an enduring one, and is regularly revisited in the news media at certain milestones on the calendar. It was when he became that ''walking footnote.''

On March 2, 1962, in Hershey, Pa., Ruklick's teammate on the Philadelphia Warriors, Wilt Chamberlain, scored the still-astonishing total of 100 points against the Knicks. For 40 years, the record has not been approached.

Ruklick's contribution to the record? He threw the pass to Chamberlain with 46 seconds left in the game for the basket that resulted in the 99th and 100th points.

Ruklick was Chamberlain's ''caddie,'' as they called the team's backup center, and had been for three years, since his rookie season. Ruklick did little backing up, though, especially in that 1961-62 season, because Chamberlain averaged a record 48.5 minutes that season; he played the full 48 minutes of every game and most of the minutes of overtime games as well.

''Frank McGuire, our coach, had some of the scrubs in during the closing minute of that game in Hershey,'' Ruklick recalled. ''York Larese, Ted Luckenbill, me. Paul Arizin, Tom Meschery, Ed Conlin -- some of the regulars -- were sitting. I'm bringing the ball up court, trying to dribble and chew gum at the same time -- not easy for me -- while Wilt's going for 100.''

Ruklick passed to Guy Rodgers, who passed to Wilt close to the basket. Wilt missed the shot. Luckenbill rebounded and passed to Wilt. Wilt missed again. Luckenbill again rebounded and this time passed to Ruklick.

Ruklick had the ball. He had been sent in to the game with orders to pass to Wilt, and Wilt only. Do not shoot. Ruklick did as instructed.

He hit Wilt with a pass in front of the basket, and the 7-2 Chamberlain spun and slammed down his monster Dipper Dunk.

The crowd of 4,000 in the Hershey Sports Arena had been chanting for Chamberlain to get 100. When he did, the fans erupted.

''I ran to the scorer's table,'' Ruklick said. ''I wanted to make sure they got that assist down. I knew I was going to be part of a once-in-a-lifetime moment. And I have -- a walking footnote.''

The Defender's sports editor, Larry Gross, recalls telling Ruklick one day in 1999 that he had heard on ESPN Radio that the ball with which Chamberlain scored the 100th point was being auctioned. A man in Annville, Pa., said he had the ball and was being interviewed.

Ruklick said: ''I called in and introduced myself and said that I knew who had the ball and this guy in Pennsylvania wasn't the one. The guy was on the air. He hung up the phone.''

Al Attles has the ball; Wilt gave it to him a few years after the game, Ruklick said, although Attles, when asked about the ball in 1999, said, ''All I know is, there's a ball somewhere, but I don't have it.''

Chamberlain once said that Attles, who had a terrific game himself, shooting a perfect 8 for 8 from the field and 1 for 1 from the foul line, ''was crying so much that he didn't get any attention that I gave the ball to him.''

Ruklick laughed. In some ways, it was Chamberlain's influence, his friendship, that made Ruklick comfortable in a black setting, that gave him a greater understanding of African-American culture.

Ruklick was born in Chicago, but when he was 11 his mother came down with tuberculosis and he was sent to an orphanage in the small town of Princeton, Ill. He found his métier, basketball, and was an all-state player.

As a senior, he was invited to the lone high school all-star game in that era, played in Murray, Ky. Chamberlain was also a senior, at Overbrook High in Philadelphia, and was considered the nation's best high school player. Ruklick looked forward to meeting Chamberlain and was shocked to learn that Chamberlain had not been invited. In 1955, black players could not play with whites in the South. ''I couldn't believe it,'' Ruklick said.
