 ATLANTA 1996 -- BADMINTON IN INDONESIA;This Is No Picnic: In Southeast Asia, Respect Rides on a Shuttlecock 
 By CHRISTOPHER CLAREY JUNE 25, 1996 
 Continue reading the main story 
 Share This Page 
 Continue reading the main story 
 Nobody knows precisely how many Indonesians were lining the streets of this massive, sprawling city when Susi Susanti and Alan Budi Kusuma came home in August 1992. Some estimates put the crowd at 500,000; others at more than a million. But this much is certain. As Susanti and Budi Kusuma stood in an open car with leis and Olympic gold medals around their necks and inched toward the national monument in Merdeka square, they had a new appreciation for the power of the very subtle game of badminton. On Sudirman, our main street, we could hardly move; it was jammed with people all shouting congratulations," Budi Kusuma said. "For five or six hours, we just kept going and going. It was like in Europe for the soccer championship or Brazil for the World Cup." Indonesia, the world's fourth most populous nation with an estimated 190 million inhabitants, does not produce renowned athletes in major professional sports like soccer and basketball or in glamorous Olympic sports like gymnastics or track and field. But badminton, known here as "bulutangkis," is a very different matter. And when all of the players on its national team are swatting shuttlecocks at the country's training facility, it is the greatest collection of badminton talent under one roof on the planet.

 There is Susanti, the intensely competitive 25-year-old considered one of the finest female players in history. She has the muscular legs of a beach volleyball star and the flexibility of a ballerina (she lunges for drop shots by doing splits). There are Ricky Subagja and Rexy Mainaky, the reigning men's doubles world champions and Olympic favorites this summer. There are also Budi Kusuma, who happens to be Susanti's fiance, and Hariyanto Arbi, the baby-faced powerhouse who won the last men's world title, and three other men ranked in the top 10 in the world.  Nearly four years after Budi Kusuma and Susanti won the first Olympic medals in the 50-year history of Indonesia, the throngs of people were missing on a trip to the training center in the southern Jakarta suburb of Cipayung. Instead, stray chickens and stray garbage lined the narrow unpaved streets that led into a large, landscaped compound with barbed wire on its high walls. "You would never know this was here unless you were looking for it," Budi Kusuma said. The Indonesians chose this unlikely spot for the training center in November 1992 because they wanted more space and privacy than was available in congested central Jakarta. "The players are better here," said Indra Gunawan, the head coach of the Indonesian badminton team. "Before, they go to the movies or go out and maybe not concentrate. When it's far from the city, they do nothing but badminton." Training Is Life; Life Is Training Sunday, even in a largely Muslim nation, is the only day of rest for the approximately 80 elite players and 18 coaches who are based here. When they are not competing in tournaments, the players train six days a week for as much as eight hours a day in an enormous hall that resembles a converted airplane hangar. They sleep in two air-conditioned dormitories -- one for women and one for men -- behind the training hall and eat in a cafeteria equipped with video games, giant-screen satellite television and a pool table. It is, in essence, a gilded cage. Players must train here if they wish to compete internationally for Indonesia and only a few players, normally those who are married, are permitted to live outside the compound. "This is our home," said Susanti, who comes from a small town in West Java and is one of the rare players not required to share her dorm room. "I go back to see my parents only twice a year." She does not express doubts about this arrangement, however. "Badminton is the only sport right now that can bring Indonesia's name to the world," Susanti said. "When Alan and I got gold medals in singles in Barcelona, the world knew Indonesia better. I am very proud about that."

 Still, much of the world took virtually no notice. Badminton is a major sport throughout southeast and east Asia. But, outside of Denmark, the rest of the west has yet to wake up to it. The sport has not always been so disconnected. The modern version of badminton is based on the Indian game poona, refined and codified in England in the late 19th century. The sport got its name because it was a popular activity at the Duke of Beaufort's country seat: Badminton House. It soon became fashionable in the United States and in the 1950's and 1960's, Americans made a habit of winning world titles. Joe Alston was considered an intriguing enough player for a new magazine called Sports Illustrated to put him on its cover in March 1955. But with the rise of network television and the major team sports, badminton receded far into the shadows, and by 1995 the United States had only about 3,000 registered players. Susanti, like others in her sport, is optimistic that badminton's inclusion in the Olympics in 1992 will ultimately broaden its reach.

 "After Atlanta, maybe America will know badminton better," Susanti said. But for America to know badminton better, it will have to get the opportunity to watch it on television, and that is certainly not part of NBC's master plan this summer. What viewers will miss is a remarkably fast and often spectacular indoor game that has little to do with the summer variant lazily played in backyards and loosely associated with straw boaters and unmuscular men. "I always maintain that top-level badminton makes top tennis players look pretty wimpish," said the International Badminton Federation's executive director David Shaw. The Indonesians' rigorous training regimen includes an exercise called "shadow badminton" in which they go through the motions of their sport in a sand pit. "Our bodies are not as big as the Europeans," Budi Kusuma said, "so we have to be faster and stronger." The Search for Talent Is an Unending Pursuit The Indonesians' advantage is that their omnipresent national federation, known by its Indonesian abbreviation of PBSI, covers all their expenses and, with help from corporate sponsors, operates 37 regional training centers in this far-flung archipelago. Talented youths are identified early by scouts, and the most promising end up in Cipayung in their late teens, although some, like Susanti and the latest female phenom, Mia Audina, arrive as early as age 15. Rich and powerful clubs like Jaya Raya in Jakarta also recruit young players and pay stars like Susanti handsomely to represent them. Despite Indonesia's regional emphasis, elite badminton is almost exclusively played in Java, which, with 110 million people, is easily the most populous of the country's 13,677 islands. The top players, including Susanti and Budi Kusuma, are almost all ethnic Chinese, a minority group that has often faced discrimination in Indonesia but has considerable economic clout. "If you ask me why, I'd say it's because badminton is a sport that not every family can afford to play," said Rudy Hartono, the greatest men's player in Indonesian history. "Playing outdoors costs little, but indoors can be expensive."

 Resentment occasionally surfaces. But the players are quick to play down the significance of their ethnic origins and point to Joko Suprianto, a male player of Javan descent currently ranked No. 1 in the world, as evidence that the sport's base is broadening. Badminton arrived here in the 1930's while Indonesia was still a Dutch colony, supposedly after Chinese traders brought the game to the island of Sumatra. But the sport did not really take flight until 13 years after independence in 1958, when Ferry Sonneville and Tan Joe Hock led Indonesia to victory in the Thomas Cup, badminton's version of the Davis Cup. It was the first major international sporting success for the new nation. Indonesia would go on to produce many top players, the best being Hartono, who would win the prestigious All-England singles title a record eight times before retiring as a national hero in 1982. After a long slump, during which the Chinese were dominant, the Indonesians came roaring back in the early 1990's under the energetic leadership of Gen. Try Sutrisno, the former PBSI president. Sutrisno, with his powerful connections in a country dominated by the military, proved particularly adept at encouraging sponsorship. He is now vice president of Indonesia, which certainly should not hurt sponsorship in the future. Success and Prosperity Heighten Expectations Susanti and Budi Kusuma, a longtime couple who plan to be married in a Catholic ceremony next February, are among those who have benefited most. They each reportedly received more than $500,000 from sponsors and government sources for their Barcelona victories: a huge payday in a nation where the average income is less than $1,000. The players' parking lot at Cipayung is filled with new Toyotas, Hondas and Opels. But Indonesia's best still cannot approach the earnings of other top international athletes. Arbi, ranked No. 1 in the world in 1995, probably earned no more than $200,000 from all sources last year. That is less than tennis stars Andre Agassi and Boris Becker can make in a week. Will it remain so in the future? Or will Asia's burgeoning affluence do for badminton in the 21st century what the West's affluence did for tennis in the 20th? There are signs of movement. In June 1994, the International Badminton Federation signed a 10-year deal with Star Television, the Asian satellite broadcaster, which guarantees increased exposure and prize money. The Indonesian players are excited, but they know, too, that greater rewards mean greater competition. The South Koreans, Danes and, in particular, the Chinese are very strong, and even Susanti is hardly a sure bet to defend her title in Atlanta. Even so, the Indonesian people are already anticipating another celebration and, above all, another opportunity to remind the world that their huge, oft-ignored nation exists.
