The men's marathon at the [[1904 Summer Olympics]] in [[St. Louis, Missouri|St. Louis]], Missouri, United States, took place on August 30 of that year, over a distance of 24 miles 1500 yards (40 km).<ref>{{cite news |last=Longman |first=Jeré |author-link=Not Without Hope |title=The Marathon's Random Route to Its Length |newspaper=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2012/04/21/sports/the-marathons-accidental-route-to-26-miles-385-yards.html?_r=0 |access-date=3 February 2016 |department=On Olympics |date=April 20, 2012 |archive-date=June 14, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170614180807/http://www.nytimes.com/2012/04/21/sports/the-marathons-accidental-route-to-26-miles-385-yards.html?_r=0 |url-status=live}}</ref>

The race was run during the hottest part of the day on dusty country roads with minimal water supply. While 32 athletes coming from seven nations (the United States, France, Cuba, Greece, the [[Orange River Colony]], Great Britain, and Canada) competed, only 14 managed to complete the race,<ref name="SR">{{cite web |title=Athletics at the 1904 St. Louis Summer Games: Men's Marathon |url=https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1904/ATH/mens-marathon.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200417171704/https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1904/ATH/mens-marathon.html |archive-date=17 April 2020 |access-date=22 July 2017 |work=sports-reference.com}}</ref> which was a bizarre affair due to poor organization and officiating.<ref name="CBOTO">{{cite book |author=Wallechinsky, David |title=The Complete Book of the Olympics |publisher=Penguin Books |year=1984 |isbn=0140066322 |location=New York |pages=44–45}}</ref> 

[[Frederick Lorz]] was greeted as the apparent winner, but was later disqualified as he had hitched a ride in a car for part of the race. The actual winner, [[Thomas Hicks (athlete)|Thomas Hicks]], was near collapse and hallucinating by the end of the race, a side effect of being administered brandy, raw eggs, and [[strychnine]] by his trainers. The fourth-place finisher, [[Andarín Carvajal]], took a nap during the race after eating spoiled apples.

==Background==
[[File:1904 Olympic Marathon participants, Len Tau (left) and Jan Mashiani of the Tswana tribe of South Africa.jpg|alt=1904 marathon participants Tau and Mashiani stand in a stadium looking at the camera, both wearing hats|thumb|200x200px|Mashiani (left) and Taunyane before the race<ref name=":1">{{Cite journal |last=van der Merwe |first=Floris J.G. |date=1999 |title=Africa's First Encounter with the Olympic Games In....1904 |url=https://isoh.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/5.pdf |journal=[[Journal of Olympic History]] |volume=September 1999 |via=International Society of Olympic Historians – ISOH |access-date=May 22, 2021 |archive-date=May 22, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210522125200/http://isoh.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/5.pdf |url-status=live}}</ref>]]

This was the third appearance of the [[Marathons at the Olympics|marathon event]], which is one of 12 athletics events to have been held at every Summer Olympics. [[Arthur L. Newton]] of the United States was the only runner from 1900 to return, while other significant American runners included the winners of the past three [[Boston Marathon]]s: 1902 winner [[Sammy Mellor]], 1903 winner [[John Lordon]], and 1904 winner [[Michael Spring]].<ref name="Olympedia">{{cite web |url=https://www.olympedia.org/results/56131 |work=Olympedia |title=Marathon, Men |access-date=23 August 2020 |archive-date=November 3, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201103051804/https://www.olympedia.org/results/56131 |url-status=live}}</ref>

Cuba and South Africa each made their first appearance in the event, while the United States was the only nation to have runners in each of the first three Olympic marathons.

The marathon included the first two black people to compete in the Olympics: two [[Tswana people|Tswana]] men named [[Len Tau|Len Taunyane]] and [[Jan Mashiani]], who happened to be in St. Louis as part of the South African exhibit at the [[1904 World's Fair]].<ref name="Smithsonian" /> Both had served as long-distance message runners during the then-recent [[Second Boer War]].<ref name=":0">{{Cite book |last1=Matthews |first1=George R. |title=St. Louis Olympics 1904 |last2=Marshall |first2=Sandra |publisher=Arcadia Pub |year=2003 |isbn=0-7385-2329-1 |location=Chicago, IL |oclc=52447869 }}</ref> Although some accounts report that both ran barefoot, Mashiani was wearing shoes in photographs taken during the event.<ref name=":1" /><ref name=":0" />

==Competition format==
The [[marathon]] distance had not yet been standardized; in St. Louis, the course was 24 miles and 1500 yards (40 km). The organizers started the marathon at 3:00 pm, whereas most modern marathons start in the early morning to take advantage of cooler times of day. 

The start included five laps, or 1{{frac|2|3}} miles (2.68 km), around the stadium track.<ref name="Olympedia" /> The remainder of the course was on dusty country roads, with race officials riding in vehicles ahead of and behind the runners: this created dust clouds that exacerbated the severely hot and humid conditions,<ref name="Smithsonian" /> with a temperature of around 90 degrees Fahrenheit (32 degrees Celsius) at the starting time.<ref name=":0" /><ref>{{Cite news |date=1904-08-28 |title=Fleetest Runners of All Nations Start in Great Marathon Race |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/st-louis-post-dispatch-04-st-louis-8-28/9311449/ |access-date=2024-02-11 |work=St. Louis Post-Dispatch |pages=27}}</ref> The route had to be altered at the last minute after roads in the [[Creve Coeur, Missouri|Creve Coeur]] area were washed out by rain.<ref>[https://www.newspapers.com/clip/99809100/ "Marathon Course Changed; Recent Rains Caused Washouts of Many Highways"], ''St. Louis Republic'', August 24, 1904, page 9.</ref>

Also, the course was not cleared of obstacles for the marathon; the runners had to dodge cross-town traffic, delivery wagons, railroad trains, trolley cars and even people walking their dogs.<ref name="Smithsonian" />

== Summary ==
[[File:Carte Marathon St-Louis-1904 EN.svg|thumb|288x288px|Marathon route]]
During the race, [[John Lordan]], who had won the 1903 Boston Marathon, was violently ill after {{convert|10|mi}} and retired, while [[Samuel Mellor|Sam Mellor]], who had won the 1902 Boston Marathon, was also overcome by the dust; despite leading the field at the halfway mark, Mellor became disoriented and ultimately dropped out of the race after {{convert|14.5|mi}}.<ref name=":0" /> Another near-fatality during the event was [[William Garcia]] of the [[United States]]. He was found lying on the road along the marathon course unconscious, with severe internal injuries that had been caused by breathing the clouds of dust kicked up by the race officials' cars.<ref name="CBOTO" />

The first to arrive at the finish line, after three hours and 13 minutes – more than 13 minutes slower than the winning time in 1900 – was [[Frederick Lorz]]. After being hailed as the winner, he had his photograph taken with [[Alice Roosevelt Longworth|Alice Roosevelt]], daughter of then-U.S. President [[Theodore Roosevelt]]: she placed a wreath upon Lorz's head, and was about to award him the gold medal when spectators claimed Lorz had not run the entire race. Lorz, suffering cramps, had actually dropped out of the race after nine miles and hitched a ride back to the stadium in a car, waving at spectators and runners alike during the ride. When the car broke down at the 19th mile, he re-entered the race and jogged across the finish line.<ref name="Smithsonian" />
[[File:Fred Lorz Cartoon.png|thumb|A 1904 editorial cartoon depicting Lorz riding a car during the marathon]]
Upon being confronted by furious race officials with these allegations, Lorz immediately admitted his deception. Despite his claim that he was playing a practical joke, the [[Amateur Athletic Union|AAU]] responded by banning Lorz for life; this was commuted to six months on February 19, 1905, after Lorz formally apologized and it was found that he had not intended to defraud. Lorz later won the 1905 [[Boston Marathon]].<ref name="CBOTO" /><ref name="LAT">{{cite news |last=Cronin |first=Brian |title=Sports Legend Revealed: A marathon runner nearly died |url=https://latimesblogs.latimes.com/sports_blog/2010/08/sports-legend-revealed-a-marathon-runner-nearly-died-because-of-drugs-he-took-to-help-him-win.html |newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]] |date=August 10, 2010 |author5=The Fabulous Forum |access-date=24 May 2024 |archive-date=October 16, 2011 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111016041131/http://latimesblogs.latimes.com/sports_blog/2010/08/sports-legend-revealed-a-marathon-runner-nearly-died-because-of-drugs-he-took-to-help-him-win.html |url-status=live}}</ref>
[[File:1904 Olympics- Thomas J. Hicks, Boston, winner of the Marathon Race resting in a car after the finish.jpg|thumb|Hicks resting after his victory|alt=A gaunt man sits in an automobile looking exhausted, with a group of men surrounding him, looking considerably healthier. All are looking at the camera.]]

[[Thomas Hicks (athlete)|Thomas Hicks]] ended up winning the event, although he benefited from various forms of aid that would not be allowed today.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://olympics.com/ioc/1967-creation-of-the-ioc-medical-commission |title=1967: Creation of the IOC Medical Commission |website=olympics.com}}</ref> {{convert|10|mi}} from the finish, Hicks led the race by {{convert|1.5|mi}}, but he had to be restrained from stopping and lying down by his trainers. From then until the end of the race, Hicks received several doses of [[strychnine]] – a common rat poison, which stimulates the nervous system in small doses – mixed with brandy and egg white.<ref name="CBOTO" /> This was the earliest recorded use of [[performance-enhancing drugs]] in the revived Olympic Games.<ref name="Smithsonian" /> He continued to battle onwards, hallucinating, and was barely able to walk for most of the course. When he reached the stadium, his support team carried him over the line, holding him in the air while he shuffled his feet as if still running.<ref name="Smithsonian" /> Hicks had to be carried off the track on a stretcher, and might have died in the stadium had he not been treated by four doctors. He lost {{convert|8|lb}} during the course of the marathon.<ref name="Smithsonian" />

Cuban postman [[Andarín Carvajal]] had also joined the marathon, arriving at the last minute.<ref name="Smithsonian" /> After losing all of his money gambling in [[New Orleans, Louisiana]], he hitchhiked to St. Louis and had to run the event in street clothes that he cut around the legs to make them into [[shorts]]. Not having eaten in 40 hours, he saw a spectator eating two peaches. He asked if he could have the peaches, and the spectator declined. He then stole both peaches and ran away. Later, he stopped off in an orchard en route to eat some apples, which turned out to be rotten.<ref name="Smithsonian" /> The rotten apples caused him to have strong stomach cramps, and he had to lie down and take a nap. Despite his discomfort and the pause, Carvajal still managed to finish in fourth place.<ref name="Smithsonian" /><ref>{{cite book |last1=Martin |first1=David E. |last2=Gynn |first2=Roger W. H. |title=The Olympic Marathon |publisher=Human Kinetics |year=2000 |isbn=978-0-88011-969-6 |page=[https://archive.org/details/olympicmarathon00mart/page/50 50] |url=https://archive.org/details/olympicmarathon00mart |url-access=registration |via=Internet Archive}}</ref>

Arriving without correct documents, [[Albert Corey]], a French immigrant to the United States, is inconsistently listed as participating in a mixed team in the four mile team race (with four undisputed Americans) and competing for the United States in the marathon.<ref name="LAT" /> The South African entrants, [[Len Taunyane]] and [[Jan Mashiani]], finished ninth and twelfth, respectively. Taunyane was chased roughly a mile off course by a dog, which affected his placement.<ref name="Smithsonian" /><ref>{{Cite web |date=February 27, 2025 |title=olympics.com |url=https://www.olympics.com/ioc/news/the-strange-case-of-the-st-louis-1904-marathon |access-date=June 3, 2026 |website=Olympics}}</ref>

==Allegations of cheating by Hicks==
Historian [[Clément Genty]] has argued, based on his own research, that the marathon's final classification should be revised. Citing the two official 1905 reports of the race — those of race director [[Charles J. P. Lucas]] and of [[James Edward Sullivan]] for the ''Spalding's Athletic Almanac'' — Genty contends that Hicks received a car escort and was physically carried by two officials over a substantial part of the final stretch, in addition to being administered strychnine mixed with egg white and brandy, despite race organizers having explicitly announced before the start that no assistance to runners would be permitted.<ref name="genty-joh">{{cite journal |last=Genty |first=Clément |title=The 1904 Olympic Marathon, an unfinished story? Nationalism at any price: from the media construction to the practical realization, the cheating at the Olympic Marathon in 1904 |journal=Journal of Olympic History |volume=31 |issue=3 |year=2023 |pages=28–32 |issn=1085-5165}}</ref><ref name="genty-cbeh">{{cite journal |last=Genty |first=Clément |title=Le Marathon olympique de 1904 : entre organisation hasardeuse et triche assumée, Albert Corey, Murisaltien pris dans l'étau de la diplomatie sportive |journal=Recueil des Travaux du CBEH |publisher=Centre Beaunois d'Études Historiques |volume=42 |year=2024 |issn=1623-4677}}</ref>

Genty draws a parallel with the disqualification of Lorz for comparable assistance, as well as with the [[Athletics at the 1908 Summer Olympics – Men's marathon|1908 Olympic marathon]], where American officials filed a protest over the assistance that first-place finisher [[Dorando Pietri]] had received from British officials, resulting in runner-up [[Johnny Hayes|John Hayes]] being declared the winner instead. Genty contrasts this with the 1904 case, in which American officials themselves had provided the assistance to Hicks without any protest being upheld, and argues that the gold medal should be retroactively awarded to [[Albert Corey]].<ref name="genty-cbeh" />

In 2023, Genty petitioned the [[International Olympic Committee]]'s Olympic Studies Centre for a formal review of the result; the Centre replied that it was not competent to revisit decisions made on the field of play or to revise the classification of a competition held nearly 120 years earlier. Genty has since raised the possibility of an appeal to the [[Court of Arbitration for Sport]] or a diplomatic approach through the French National Olympic and Sports Committee.<ref name="genty-cbeh" /> {{As of|2026}}, the official classification of the event has not been revised.

==Dehydration==
The only source of water for the competitors was a well at about the halfway ({{convert|12|mi|750|yd}}) mark. However Carvajal managed to get a drink from a water tower roughly {{convert|6|mi}} in.<ref name="CBOTO" /><ref name="Smithsonian">{{cite magazine |last=Abbott |first=Karen |author-link=Karen Abbott |date=August 7, 2012 |title=The 1904 Olympic Marathon May Have Been The Strangest Ever |department=History |url=https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-1904-olympic-marathon-may-have-been-the-strangest-ever-14910747 |url-status=live |magazine=[[Smithsonian (magazine)|Smithsonian]] |access-date=24 May 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140209105905/http://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-1904-olympic-marathon-may-have-been-the-strangest-ever-14910747/ |archive-date=February 9, 2014}}</ref> [[James Edward Sullivan]] was a chief organizer of the Olympics, and set up no other water sources along the 24 miles and 1500 yards course of the marathon even though it was conducted in {{convert|32|C|F}} heat over unpaved roads that were choked with dust. His ostensible reason was to conduct research on "purposeful [[dehydration]]": this, combined with poor officiating, saw the marathon end with the worst ratio of finishers to starters (14 out of 32), and the slowest winning time, 3:28:53, which was exactly 29 minutes slower than the second-slowest winning time.<ref name="Smithsonian" />
