Phelps finished the 100-meter butterfly Saturday morning (Friday night ET) with a half-stroke that looked like a mistake and, on some replays, like a loss, but turned into gold medal No. 7 in his Beijing Olympic collection.
"It's almost too close to see," Phelps admitted of his winning margin of one-hundredth of a second over Serbia's Milorad Cavic, which sparked a protest and video review but was upheld.
Phelps has just one more swim left, in the 4x100-meter medley relay on Sunday morning (Saturday night ET) in his quest to surpass Spitz's mark from the 1972 Games.
"I saw so many quotes saying it's impossible to duplicate it, it won't happen, and it just shows you that really anything can happen," Phelps said of tying the record.
Knowing that, Phelps still isn't ready to relax, even though the U.S. men have never lost an Olympic medley relay. Australia appears to be the top challenger here.
If the USA keeps its winning streak alive, it will crown the single greatest Olympic performance of all time for Phelps.
"It seems like every day, I'm in sort of a dream world," Phelps said. "I'm just happy I'm in the real world."
Entering Saturday's 100 fly final, Phelps had set world records while winning his four other individual events in Beijing, some by jaw-dropping margins.
The 100 fly, though, is the one event Phelps swims in which he doesn't hold the world record. That belongs to U.S. teammate Ian Crocker, whom Phelps narrowly beat at the 2004 Olympics and at last year's world championships.
On Saturday, Crocker was on one side of Phelps and Cavic, the fastest qualifier, was on the other. At the 50-meter turn, Phelps, who has more closing strength than starting speed, was seventh. Cavic was first and Crocker second.
Phelps churned to reel them in, and Crocker, who finished fourth, faded. As they approached the finish, Cavic still was slightly ahead. Cavic began his glide into the wall, arms out straight underwater, and Phelps made a split-second decision to take a fast half-stroke, although a glide is usually the more effective finish.
"I really thought that cost me the race, but it happened to be the direct opposite," Phelps said.
Phelps turned to the scoreboard and thought he saw a '1' next to his name, meaning he had finished first. He had to take off his goggles to be sure. When he was, he roared and pounded his fists in the water.
"It was a little bit of everything — relief, excitement," he said of his reaction of seeing he had finished in 50.58 seconds — not a world record but a personal-best time and just ahead of Cavic's 50.59.
Nearby, U.S. national team coach Mark Schubert was standing near the Serbian coaches. He heard them talking about filing a protest of the result with FINA, swimming's international governing body. He followed them to the officials' room, where they initially just questioned whether the electronic timing system was working properly, he said.
FINA officials told the Serbians that the system had been checked and was working and that the race referee, Ben Ekumbo, had checked the video backup, which can be slowed, frame by frame, to ten-thousandths of a second.
"It was very clear that the Serbian swimmer touched second after Michael Phelps," Ekumbo told reporters. "It was clear from the video it was a matter of stroking. One was stroking (Phelps) and the other was gliding."
Schubert said U.S. coaches often remind swimmers that they need to touch the wall hard enough to turn off the timing system. Cavic going into the wall with a soft glide as opposed to Phelps' forceful stroke likely was the difference in the race, he said.
The Serbians filed an official protest in writing after the 100 fly awards ceremony, Schubert said, so that they could watch the video. FINA rules don't allow coaches to view video unless they file a protest. They watched the video and agreed with Ekumbo's ruling.
Cavic, who was born in California to Serbian parents and swam at Cal before training the last year and a half with U.S. sprinter Gary Hall Jr. in Florida, didn't question the result although he did say, "I think if we got to do this again, I would win."
"I'm very, very happy," Cavic said. "I don't want to fight this. It is a gold medal at stake. It's a difficult thing to lose. But you have to understand I came to this competition with the goal to win a bronze medal. I went my best time, and I did better than bronze, I got silver. And I almost got gold."
Cavic did fuel some fires before the race, saying Friday that "it would be good for the sport" if Phelps lost. Phelps' coach, Bob Bowman, heard about the quote and decided to tell Phelps while they were walking to breakfast early Saturday morning.
"I wasn't going to (tell Phelps) at first," Bowman said, "and I was thinking to myself, 'You know, this race is going to be very tight and I'm going to use everything I got.' So I put it out there. Maybe it was worth a hundredth."
