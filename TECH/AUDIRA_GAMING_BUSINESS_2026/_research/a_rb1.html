Roblox paid creators $1.5 billion in 2025

By James Hale • 09/03/2026 •
Photo by Hannah Peters/Getty Images

Roblox says U.S.-based creators making games and purchasable items on its platform had a GDP impact of $752 million in 2025–a 69% jump from what they generated in 2024.

That means in 2025, the Roblox ecosystem supported the equivalent of 7,525 full-time jobs in the States (and “nearly 12,000” globally, the company said). This too was a jump–up 55% from 2024.

The data comes from Roblox’s latest series of Economic Impact Reports, which show how much the platform is financially affecting creators in the U.S. and a handful of other major countries and regions.

“The creators behind the games, avatars, and accessories on Roblox live all around the world, many far from cities where gaming and technology jobs are common,” Roblox said in the report write-up. “The Roblox platform fuels a robust creator economy where people earn real income from the games and items they create.”

It added that in 2025, Roblox creators across the globe collectively earned more than $1.5 billion from their cuts of game and item sales. For the third time, this is a jump from 2024’s total of $923 million.

About $673 million of that $1.5 billion went to creators in the U.S. And of the payments made to U.S. creators, 66% of them (~$444 million) went to people who live in “areas where the concentration of tech workers was lower than the national average,” per the report.

Roblox said this stat proves it “creates opportunities across many different communities in the U.S.”

It cites two U.S.-based creators–North Carolina-based game developer Henry Hollyday and top avatar accessories maker Rush Bogin–as examples of creators who have built full-scale businesses on Roblox revenue alone.

Both creators concurred; Hollyday said Roblox “opened the door for me to start making games and earning a living from them,” while Bogin mentioned that “[b]uilding a brand, managing partnerships, even handling taxes—there’s so much I’ve learned about running a business that I never expected to learn just from creating on Roblox.”

In total, from 2017 to 2025, Roblox created 2017 to 2025, Roblox generated a cumulative estimated GDP impact of $2.37 billion in the U.S. alone, it said.

It’s clearly expecting the uptrend in creator earnings to continue from 2025 to 2026 and beyond, because it’s investing in creator tools. “To enable long-term creator success, we continually optimize and add to the tools and features that facilitate game development and monetization,” Roblox said.

One tool it chose to highlight is its vibe coding feature Build, where users can attempt to make their own games by feeding text prompts into a gen AI chatbot. (We’re expecting an eventual large-scale rollout of that feature will put Roblox squarely in the same slop deluge crossroads YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and other platforms-owned-by-AI-hungry-big-tech-companies have been dealing with for months now.)

Roblox also mentioned its Jumpstart and Incubator programs, which are aimed at getting more creators making stuff (and generating money).

We don’t disagree with Roblox here; we think its creator revenue will continue to rise. Roblox is a behemoth of kids’ gaming content, and as Roblox demonstrated above, is a platform where individual developers, group studios, and small businesses can earn significant cash and build a solid user base.

All that being said, its progress may be hindered by intense scrutiny from U.S. regulators and a growing crowd of lawsuits. It’s been sued by nearly a dozen states, the latest of them Ohio, which accuses the platform of being an “unregulated digital environment where child predators and sexual content [flourish].”

Numerous parents have filed suit too. Some of them lost children to suicide. Others have children who were sexually abused by people they met through Roblox.

One recent case was filed by the parents of a 13-year-old who committed suicide after allegedly being exposed to “harmful and violent influences” on Roblox. Roblox moved for the suit to be dismissed, claiming the child (who signed up for an account when she was just eight years old) waived her right to legal action by agreeing to its TOS.

So, yes, again, there’s no question Roblox is facilitating significant earnings for creators–and itself–and that those earnings rise each year. But if unrest over its child safety efforts continues, it might struggle to keep that trend going.
