The '''Seven Sleepers''' ({{Langx|el|ἑπτὰ κοιμώμενοι|hepta koimōmenoi}};<ref>{{Cite book |title= Κοράνιον |trans-title= The Koran |chapter= Chapter 18 |translator-last= Pentakis |translator-first= Gerasimos I.  |year= 1886 |edition= reprint |publisher= Printing House of the Establishments of A. [Anestis] Konstantinidis (Τυπογραφείον των καταστημάτων Α. Κωνσταντινίδου) |location= Athens |page= 210 |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=A9s7AQAAMAAJ&pg=PA210 |access-date= 7 September 2026}}</ref> {{Langx|la|Septem dormientes}}), also known in [[Christianity]] as the '''Seven Sleepers of Ephesus''', and in [[Islam]] as '''Aṣḥāb al-Kahf''' (اصحاب الکهف, ''aṣḥāb al-kahf'', lit. '''Companions of the Cave'''),<ref name= Archer>{{cite journal |last=Archer |first=George |date=October 2016 |title=The Hellhound of the Qur'an: A Dog at the Gate of the Underworld |journal=[[Journal of Qur'anic Studies]] |location=[[Edinburgh]] |publisher=[[Edinburgh University Press]] on behalf of the Centre for Islamic Studies at the [[School of Oriental and African Studies]] |volume=18 |issue=3 |pages=1–33 |doi=10.3366/jqs.2016.0248 |eissn=1755-1730 |issn=1465-3591 |oclc=43733991}}</ref> is a [[Late antiquity|late antique]] [[Christianity|Christian]] legend, and a [[Qur'anic]] [[Islam]]ic story. It speaks about a group of youths who hid inside a cave around the year 250 AD to escape [[Persecution of Christians in the Roman Empire|Roman persecutions of Christians]] and emerged many years later.<ref name= madain>{{cite web |title=Cave of Ashabe Kahf (The Cave of the Seven Sleepers) |website= Madain Project |url=https://madainproject.com/cave_of_ashab_e_kahf |access-date=2 November 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201102114623/https://madainproject.com/cave_of_ashab_e_kahf |archive-date=2 November 2020}}</ref> The Qur'anic version of the story appears in [[Sura 18]] ([[Q18:9–26|18:9–26]]).<ref name= Archer/>

The Seven Sleepers have been venerated as Christian saints since at least the 5th century as the "Holy Seven Youths" (Άγιοι Επτά Παίδες) in the Orthodox church;<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |title=Ορθόδοξος Συναξαριστής :: Άγιοι Επτά Παίδες εν Εφέσω |url=https://www.saint.gr/2218/saint.aspx |access-date=2025-03-23 |website=www.saint.gr}}</ref> in the Catholic Church, they are venerated individually.<ref name="NewAdvent">"The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus." In The Catholic Encyclopedia, Vol. 5. New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1909. Retrieved from https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05496a.htm</ref>

==Origins and propagation ==
The story appeared in several [[Syriac Christianity|Syriac]] sources before [[Gregory of Tours]]'s lifetime (538–594). The earliest Syriac manuscript copy is in MS Saint-Petersburg No. 4, which dates to the 5th century.<ref name= WW>Witold Witakowski, [https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Sleepers-of-Ephesus-Legend-of-the "Sleepers of Ephesus, Legend of the"], in ''Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage: Electronic Edition'', edited by [[Sebastian P. Brock]], Aaron M. Butts, [[George A. Kiraz]] and Lucas Van Rompay (Gorgias Press, 2011; online ed. Beth Mardutho, 2018).</ref>

The earliest known version of this story{{clarify |Contradicts the above: MS St-Pet. No. 4 is C5, and this one can even be C6 - maybe this is the oldest COMPLETE version? |date= February 2024}} is found in the writings of the Syriac bishop [[Jacob of Serugh]] ({{circa|450}}–521), who relies on an earlier Greek source, now lost.<ref name= horst/> Jacob of Serugh, an [[Edessa]]n poet-theologian, wrote a homily in verse on the subject of the Seven Sleepers,<ref name= Sabine>{{Cite web |first= S. |last= Baring-Gould |author-link= Sabine Baring-Gould |title= Curious myths of the Middle Ages |year= 1876 |publisher= Rivingtons |location= London |url= http://archive.org/details/curiousmythsofmi00bariuoft |via=Internet Archive}}</ref> which was published in the ''[[Acta Sanctorum]]''. Another sixth-century version gives eight sleepers in a Syrian manuscript in the [[British Museum]] (''Cat. Syr. Mss'', p.&nbsp;1090).

Whether the original account was written in Syriac or Greek was debated, but today a Greek original is generally accepted.<ref name= BG>Bartłomiej Grysa, [http://czasopisma.upjp2.edu.pl/orientalia/article/view/1011 "The Legend of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus in Syriac and Arab Sources: A Comparative Study"], ''Orientalia Christiana Cracoviensia'' '''2''' (2010): 45–59.</ref><ref name= WW/> The pilgrim account ''[[De situ terrae sanctae]]'', written between 518 and 531, records the existence of a church dedicated to the sleepers in Ephesus.<ref name= WW/>

An outline of this tale appears in the 6th-century writings of [[Gregory of Tours]] and the ''History of the [[Lombards]]'' of [[Paul the Deacon]] (720–799).<ref>{{cite book |last=Liuzza |first=R. M. |date=2016 |editor1-last=Kears |editor1-first=Carl |editor2-last=Paz |editor2-first=James |title=Medieval Science Fiction |chapter=The Future is a Foreign Country: The Legend of the Seven Sleepers and the Anglo–Saxon Sense of the Past |publisher=[[King's College London]], Centre for Late Antique & Medieval Studies |page=66 |isbn=978-0-9539838-8-9}}</ref> The best-known Western version of the story appears in [[Jacobus de Voragine]]'s ''[[Golden Legend]]'' (1259–1266).  It also appears in [[Bibliotheca Hagiographica Orientalis|BHO]] (''Pueri septem''),<ref>{{Cite book |last1=Peeters |first1=P. |url=http://archive.org/details/bibliothecahagio00peetuoft |title=Bibliotheca hagiographica orientalis |last2=Société de Bollandistes |date=1910 |publisher=Bruxellis, apud editores [Beyrouth (Syrie) Imprimerie catholique] |others=Robarts – University of Toronto |pages=1012–1022}}</ref> [[Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca|BHG]] (''Pueri VII'')<ref>{{Cite book |last=Bollandistes |url=http://archive.org/details/bibliothecahagi00boll |title=Bibliotheca hagiographica graeca |date=1909 |publisher=Bruxellis, Société des Bollandistes |others=PIMS – University of Toronto |pages=1593–1599}}</ref> and [[Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina|BHL]] ''Dormientes (Septem) Ephesi''.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Bollandists |url=http://archive.org/details/bibliothecahagio01boll |title=Bibliotheca hagiographica latina antiquae et mediae aetatis |date=1898 |publisher=Bruxellis: [s.n.] |others=PIMS – University of Toronto |pages=2313–2319}}</ref>

Accounts of the Christian legend are found in at least nine medieval languages and preserved in over 200 manuscripts, mainly dating to between the 9th and 13th centuries. These include 104 [[Latin]] manuscripts, 40 [[Greek language|Greek]], 33 [[Arabic]], 17 [[Syriac language|Syriac]], six [[Ethiopic]], five [[Coptic language|Coptic]], two [[Armenian language|Armenian]], one [[Middle Irish]], and one [[Old English]].<ref name= BG/><ref>[[Hugh Magennis (scholar)|Hugh Magennis]], [http://digital.library.leeds.ac.uk/298/1/LSE1991_pp43-56_Magennis_article.pdf "The Anonymous Old English Legend of the Seven Sleepers and its Latin Source"], ''Leeds Studies in English'', n.s. '''22''' (1991): 43–56.</ref> Byzantine writer [[Symeon the Metaphrast]] (died c. 1000) alluded to it.<ref name= Sabine/> It was also translated into [[Sogdian language|Sogdian]]. In the 13th century, the poet [[Chardri]] composed an [[Old French]] version.  The ninth-century Irish calendar {{lang|sga|[[Félire Óengusso]]}} commemorates the Seven Sleepers on 7 August.<ref>{{cite book |last= Stokes |first= Whitley |author-link= Whitley Stokes (Celtic scholar) |title= The Martyrology of Oengus the Culdee: Félire Óengusso Céli Dé |url= https://archive.org/details/martyrologyoeng01stokgoog |year=1905 |publisher= Harrison and Sons |page=[https://archive.org/details/martyrologyoeng01stokgoog/page/n62 4]}}</ref>

The legend was also translated into [[New Persian|Persian]], [[Kyrgyz language|Kyrgyz]], and [[Tatar language|Tatar]].<ref name= WW/>

===Dissemination in the West: story and relics===
[[File:Legendari di sancti istoriado uulgar, 1497 – (sette dormienti) - BEIC IE4411197.jpg|thumb|left|The Seven Sleepers in the ''[[Golden Legend]]'' (1497)]]

The story rapidly attained a wide diffusion throughout Christendom. It was popularized in the West by Gregory of Tours, in his late 6th-century collection of miracles, ''Glory of the Martyrs (De gloria martyrum)''.<ref name= Sabine/> Gregory claimed to have gotten the story from "a certain Syrian interpreter" (''Syro quidam interpretante''), but this could refer to either a Syriac- or Greek-speaker from the Levant.<ref name= WW/> During the period of the [[Crusades]], bones from the sepulchres near Ephesus, identified as relics of the Seven Sleepers, were transported to [[Marseille]], France, in a large stone coffin, which remained a trophy of the [[Abbey of St Victor, Marseille]].

The Seven Sleepers were included in the ''[[Golden Legend]]'' compilation, the most popular book of the later Middle Ages, which fixed a precise date for their resurrection, AD&nbsp;478, in the reign of Theodosius{{dubious| No such regnal year for any Theodosius. |date= February 2024}}.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.catholic-forum.com/saints/golden235.htm |title=The Seven Sleepers |website=The Golden Legend |url-status=dead |quote=It is in doubt of that which is said that they slept three hundred and sixty-two years, for they were raised the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight, and Decius reigned but one year and three months, and that was in the year of our Lord two hundred and seventy, and so they slept but two hundred and eight years. |archive-date=6 January 2003 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20030106115440/http://www.catholic-forum.com/saints/golden235.htm }}</ref><ref>{{cite book |author=Jacobus |editor-first=H.D. |editor-last=Madge |others=C.M. Watts (illustrator) |date=1899 |author-link=Jacobus de Voragine |title=Leaves from the Golden Legend |chapter=XV — The Seven Sleepers |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=EIMSAAAAYAAJ&pg=PA167 |pages=174–175 |quote=It is doubt of that which is said that they slept ccclxii. years. For they were raised the year of Our Lord IIIICLXXXIII. And Decius reigned but one year and three months and that was in the year of our Lord CC and LXX., and so they slept but iic. and viii. years. |via=Google Books }}</ref>

==Christian story==
[[File:Seven sleepers.jpg|thumb|A 19th&nbsp;century German votive painting of the Seven Sleepers. The writing says ''Bittet für uns Ihr hl. sieben Schläfer'' (''Pray for us, Holy Seven Sleepers'').]]

The story says that during the [[persecution of Christians|persecutions]] by the Roman emperor [[Decius]], around AD&nbsp;250, seven young men were accused of following [[Christianity]]. They were given time to recant their faith but refused to bow to Roman idols. Instead, they chose to give their worldly goods to the poor and retire to a mountain cave to pray, where they fell asleep. The Emperor, seeing that their attitude towards [[paganism]] had not improved, ordered the mouth of the cave to be sealed.<ref name= Fortescue/>
[[File:Siebenschläferkirche in Rotthof (Ruhstorf an der Rott) aussen Südseite - Siebenschläfer-Reliefs 1 und 2 - Bild 2.jpg|thumb|right|Roman [[headstone]]s misinterpreted as to show the Seven Sleepers, in a [[:de:Siebenschläferkirche (Rotthof)|Church in Rotthof, Germany, named after the legend]]]]

Decius died in 251, and many years passed during which Christianity went from being persecuted to being the [[State church of the Roman Empire|state religion of the Roman Empire]]. At some later time—usually given as during the reign of [[Theodosius II]] (408–450)—in AD 447 when heated discussions were taking place between various schools of Christianity about the resurrection of the body in the day of judgement and life after death, a landowner decided to open up the sealed mouth of the cave, thinking to use it as a cattle pen. He opened it and found the sleepers inside. They awoke, imagining that they had slept but one day, and sent one of their number to Ephesus to buy food, with instructions to be careful.<ref name= Sabine/>

Upon arriving in the city, this person was astounded to find buildings with crosses attached; the townspeople were astounded to find a man trying to spend old coins from the reign of Decius. The bishop was summoned to interview the sleepers; they told him their miracle story, and died praising God.<ref name= Fortescue/>

The Seven Sleepers' various lives in Greek and other non-Latin languages are listed at [[Bibliotheca Hagiographica Orientalis|''BHO'']].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://archive.org/details/bibliothecahagio00peetuoft|title=Bibliotheca hagiographica orientalis|first1=P.|last1=Peeters|last2=Société de Bollandistes|date=25 October 2018|publisher=Bruxellis, apud editores [Beyrouth (Syrie) Imprimerie catholique]|via=Internet Archive}}</ref>

==Islamic story==
{{main|Al-Kahf}}
[[File:Berlin,_Pertsch_Persisch_1016_fol_187r_Seven_Sleepers.jpg|thumb|Islamic [[Persian miniature]] of the Sleepers from a 1577 [[Qisas al-Anbiya|''Stories of the Prophets'']] manuscript.]]
Al-Kahf ({{langx|ar|الكهف|lit=the Cave}}) is the 18th chapter ([[sūrah]]) of the Qur'an with 110 verses ([[āyāt]]). Regarding the timing and contextual background of the revelation (''[[asbāb al-nuzūl]]''), it is an earlier [[Meccan surah]], which means it was revealed before Muhammad's [[hijrah]] to Medina instead of after. It is the midst sura of Qur'an having the midst word "walyatalattaf" (وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ), meaning "let him be kind".<ref>{{cite web |title=الدعاء عند وليتلطف في سورة الكهف لأنها منتصف القرآن|url=https://www.islamweb.net/ar/fatwa/393679/%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AF%D8%B9%D8%A7%D8%A1-%D8%B9%D9%86%D8%AF-%D9%88%D9%84%D9%8A%D8%AA%D9%84%D8%B7%D9%81-%D9%81%D9%8A-%D8%B3%D9%88%D8%B1%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%83%D9%87%D9%81-%D9%84%D8%A3%D9%86%D9%87%D8%A7-%D9%85%D9%86%D8%AA%D8%B5%D9%81-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%82%D8%B1%D8%A2%D9%86|website=www.islamweb.net|access-date=26 August 2025|language=ar}}</ref>

The precise number of sleepers is not stated. The Qur'an furthermore states that people started to make "idle guesses" as to how many people were in the cave shortly after the incident emerged. To this, the Qur'an asserts, "My Sustainer knows best how many they were."<ref>{{Cite book|title=The Message of the Quran, by M. Asad, Surah 18:22}}</ref>. However, it has been suggested to have been seven like the other Greek legends suggest<ref>{{Cite web |title=Ma'arif al-Qur'an — Tafsir Surah Al-Kahf, Ayah 22 |url=https://quran.com/al-kahf/22/tafsirs/en-tafsir-maarif-ul-quran |access-date=2026-07-29 |website=Quran.com |language=en}}</ref>, with the noted that it was one of the views of the sects of Christians at Najran, Arabia. Tafsir Ibn Kathir mentions narrations from several companions of the Prophet saying they were the ones who knew the real number, and said it was seven<ref>{{Cite web |title=Ibn Kathir (Abridged) — Tafsir Surah Al-Kahf, Ayah 22 |url=https://quran.com/al-kahf/22/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir |access-date=2026-07-29 |website=Quran.com |language=en}}</ref>, so it is unanimously agreed by most exegetes in Sunni Islam as the number being seven sleepers while the dog being the eighth. Similarly, regarding the exact period of time the people stayed in the cave, the Qur'an, after asserting the guesswork of the people that "they remained in the cave for 300 years and nine added," resolves that "God knows best how long they remained [there]."<ref>{{Cite web |title=Surah Al-Kahf Ayah 25 - Read, Listen, Translation, Tafsir |url=https://quran.com/al-kahf/25 |access-date=2026-07-29 |website=Quran.com |language=en}}</ref> The 9 years are often interpreted as the difference between solar and lunar years.<ref>{{Cite book|title=The Message of the Quran, by M. Asad, Surah 18:25–26}}</ref> The Qur'an says that the sleepers included a dog, which Islamic tradition names as [[Qitmir]], who guarded the entrance of the cave (verse 18).<ref name= Archer/>

''The Qur'an and the Bible: Text and Commentary''. Yale University Press, p. 454.</ref><ref>Tobias Nicklas in: C. R. Moss et al., eds. ===Islamic view===
The Qur'an does not state the exact number of sleepers in the cave. [[Al-Kahf#Summary|Qur'an 18:22]] discusses the disputes regarding their numbers. The verse says:

<blockquote>Some will say, "They were three, their dog was the fourth," while others will say, "They were five, their dog was the sixth," only guessing blindly. And others will say, "They were seven and their dog was the eighth." Say, O Prophet, "My Lord knows best their exact number. Only a few people know as well." So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those who debate about them.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://quran.com/18/22 | title=Surah Al-Kahf - 22 }}</ref>

Some Islamic exegetical traditions report that the People of the Cave were seven. Reports attributed to Ibn Abbas state that he was among those who knew their number and that they were seven. {{cite book |author=Ibn Kathir |title=Tafsir Ibn Kathir |chapter=Surah Al-Kahf (18:22)}}

</blockquote>

==Duration==
===Christian accounts===
The number of years the sleepers slept also varies between accounts. The highest number, given by Gregory of Tours, was 373 years. Some accounts have 372. [[Jacobus de Voragine]] calculated it at 196 (from the year 252 until 448).<ref name= BG/> Other calculations suggest 195.<ref name= WW/>

==Caves of the Seven Sleepers==
Several sites<ref name= madain/> are attributed as the "Cave of the Seven Sleepers," but none could empirically prove to be the original site associated with the legend. As the earliest versions of the legend spread out from Ephesus, an early Christian [[catacomb]] in that area came to be associated with it, attracting scores of pilgrims. On the slopes of Mount Pion (Mount Coelian) near Ephesus (near modern [[Selçuk]] in Turkey), the [[grotto]] of the Seven Sleepers with ruins of the religious site built over it was excavated in 1926–1928.<ref>[[Nancy Thomson de Grummond|de Grummond, N. T.]], ed., ''Encyclopedia of the History of Classical Archaeology'' (London & New York: Routledge, 1996), [https://books.google.com/books?id=LeE4CQAAQBAJ&pg=PA394 p. 394].</ref>{{rp|394}} The excavation brought to light several hundred graves dated to the 5th and 6th&nbsp;centuries. Inscriptions dedicated to the Seven Sleepers were found on the walls and in the graves. This grotto is still shown to tourists.

Other possible sites of the cave of the Seven Sleepers are in Damascus, Syria and [[Afşin]] and [[Tarsus, Mersin|Tarsus]], Turkey. Afşin is near the antique [[Roman Empire|Roman]] city of [[Arabissus]], to which the [[East Roman]] Emperor [[Justinian]] paid a visit. The site was a [[Hittites|Hittite]] temple, used as a Roman temple and later as a church in Roman and [[Byzantine]] times. The Emperor brought marble niches from Western Anatolia as gifts for it, which are preserved inside the [[Eshab-ı Kehf Kulliye]] mosque to this day. The [[Seljuks]] continued to use the place of worship as a church and a mosque. It was turned into a mosque over time, with the conversion of the local population to Islam.

A cave near [[Amman]], Jordan, also known as the [[Cave of Seven Sleepers]], which has eight smaller sealed tombs inside and a ventilation duct coming out of the cave.<ref name= LP>[https://www.lonelyplanet.com/jordan/amman/attractions/cave-of-the-seven-sleepers/a/poi-sig/1105549/361068 Cave of the Seven Sleepers] (at [[Lonely Planet]])</ref>

==Modern literature==
===Early modern===
[[File:Famous German story of the collection, Life of the Saints, Der Heiligen Leben, including the legend of the Seven Sleepers, 15th century CE, Germany.jpg|thumb|The famous German story of the collection "Life of the Saints" (Der Heiligen Leben), including the legend of "the Seven Sleepers," 15th century, Germany]]
[[File:The Arabic story of Companions of the Cave, Qissat Ahl el-Kahaf, 1494 CE, origin unknown.jpg|thumb|The Arabic story of the Companions of the Cave (Seven Sleepers; Qissat Ahl el-Kahaf), 1494, origin unknown]]

The account had become proverbial in 16th-century Protestant culture. The poet [[John Donne]] could ask,

<poem>
        I wonder, by my troth, what thou and I
        Did, till we loved? Were we not weaned till then?
        But sucked on country pleasures, childishly?
        Or snorted we in the Seven Sleepers' den?—John Donne, "[[The Good-Morrow]]".</poem>

In [[John Heywood]]'s ''Play called the Four PP'' (1530s), the Pardoner, a Renaissance update of the protagonist in [[Geoffrey Chaucer|Chaucer]]'s "[[The Pardoner's Tale]]," offers his companions the opportunity to kiss "a slipper / Of one of the Seven Sleepers." Still, the relic is presented as absurdly as the Pardoner's other offerings, which include "the great-toe of the Trinity" and "a buttock-bone of Pentecost."<ref>{{cite book |title=Medieval and Tudor Drama |url=https://archive.org/details/medievaltudordra00john |url-access=registration |editor-first=John |editor-last=Gassner |location=New York |publisher=Applause |year=1987 |page=[https://archive.org/details/medievaltudordra00john/page/245 245] |isbn=9780936839844}}</ref>

Little is heard of the Seven Sleepers during [[the Enlightenment]], but the account revived with the coming of [[Romanticism]]. The ''Golden Legend'' may have been the source for retellings of the Seven Sleepers in [[Thomas de Quincey]]'s ''[[Confessions of an English Opium-Eater]]'', in a poem by [[Johann Wolfgang von Goethe|Goethe]], [[Washington Irving]]'s "[[Rip van Winkle]]," and [[H. G. Wells]]'s ''[[The Sleeper Awakes]]''. It also might influence the motif of the "[[King asleep in mountain]]." [[Mark Twain]] did a burlesque of the story of the Seven Sleepers in Chapter&nbsp;13 of Volume&nbsp;2 of ''The Innocents Abroad''.<ref>{{cite book |title=The Unabridged Mark Twain |url=https://archive.org/details/unabridgedmarktw02twai |url-access=registration |author=Samuel Clements |editor=Lawrence Teacher |location=Philadelphia PA |publisher=Running Press |year=1976 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/unabridgedmarktw02twai/page/245 pp. 245–248]}}</ref>

===Contemporary===
[[Edward Gibbon]] gives different accounts of the story in ''[[The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire]]''.

The [[Serbia]]n writer [[Danilo Kiš]] retells the story of the Seven Sleepers in a short story, "The Legend of the Sleepers," from his book ''[[The Encyclopedia of the Dead]]''.

The [[Italy|Italian]] author [[Andrea Camilleri]] incorporates the story in his novel ''[[The Terracotta Dog]]'' in which the protagonist is led to a cave containing the titular watchdog (as described in the ''Qur'an'' and called "Kytmyr" in [[Sicily|Sicilian]] folklore) and the saucer of silver coins with which one of the sleepers is to buy "pure food" from the bazaar in Ephesus (''Qur'an'' 18.19). The Seven Sleepers are symbolically replaced by lovers [[Lisetta Moscato]] and [[Mario Cunich]], who were killed in their nuptial bed by an assassin hired by Lisseta's incestuous father and later laid to rest in a cave in the Sicilian countryside.

In [[Susan Cooper]]'s ''[[The Dark Is Rising]]'' series, Will Stanton awakens the Seven Sleepers in ''[[The Grey King]]'', and in ''[[Silver on the Tree]]'', they ride in the last battle against the Dark.

The ''Seven Sleepers'' series by [[Gilbert Morris]] takes a modern approach to the story in which seven teenagers must be awakened to fight evil in a post-nuclear-apocalypse world.

[[John Buchan]] refers to the Seven Sleepers in ''[[The Three Hostages]],'' in which [[Richard Hannay]] surmises that his wife Mary, a sound sleeper, is descended from one of the seven who has married one of the [[Parable of the Ten Virgins|Foolish Virgins]].

The Seven Sleepers are mentioned in the "Les Invisibles" song on the 1988 [[Blue Öyster Cult]] album [[Imaginos]].

Several languages have idioms related to the Seven Sleepers, including:
* [[Hungarian language|Hungarian]]: ''hétalvó'', literally a "seven-sleeper," or "one who sleeps for an entire week," is a colloquial reference to a person who oversleeps or who is typically drowsy.<ref>Kohler, W. C., & Kurz, P. J., ''Hypnosis in the Management of Sleep Disorders'' (London & New York: [[Routledge]], 2018), [https://books.google.com/books?id=1EcrDwAAQBAJ&pg=PA8 p. 8].</ref>{{rp|8}}
* [[Irish language|Irish]]: "Na seacht gcodlatáin" refers to hibernating animals.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/codlat%C3%A1n|title=Foclóir Gaeilge–Béarla (Ó Dónaill): codlatán|website=www.teanglann.ie}}</ref>
* [[Norwegian language|Norwegian]]: a late riser may be referred to as a ''syvsover'' ("seven sleeper")<ref>[https://web.archive.org/web/20210224195044/https://ordbok.uib.no/perl/ordbok.cgi?OPP=+syvsover&ant_bokmaal=5&ant_nynorsk=5&begge=+&ordbok=begge Entry for '''syvsover'''], ''Sprakradet'', [[Language Council of Norway]].</ref>
* [[Swedish language|Swedish]]: a late riser may be referred to as a ''sjusovare'' ("seven sleeper").<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.saob.se/artikel/?seek=sjusovare+&pz=1#U_S2777_48588|title=sju-sovare &#124; SAOB}}</ref>
* [[Welsh language|Welsh]]: a late riser may be referred to as a ''saith cysgadur'' ("seven sleeper") – as in the 1885 novel ''[[Rhys Lewis (novel)|Rhys Lewis]]'' by [[Daniel Owen]], where the protagonist is referred to as such in chapter 37, p.&nbsp;294 (Hughes a'i Fab, [[Caerdydd]], 1948).

==Feast day==
The most recent edition of the ''[[Roman Martyrology]]'' commemorates the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus under the date of 27 July.<ref>''[[Martyrologium Romanum]]'', editio altera, (Typis Vaticanis, 2004, p. 416 {{ISBN|88-209-7210-7}})</ref> The [[Byzantine calendar]] commemorates them with feasts on 4 August and 22 October. [[Syriac Orthodox]] calendars gives various dates: 21 April, 2 August, 13 August, 23 October and 24 October.<ref name= WW/>

== Pop culture ==
''[[The Men of Angelos]]'', also known as ''The Companions of the Cave'', is a 1997 [[Iranian cinema|Iranian]] television series directed by [[Farajollah Salahshoor]]. It depicts the story of the Seven Sleepers, according to the Qur'an.
