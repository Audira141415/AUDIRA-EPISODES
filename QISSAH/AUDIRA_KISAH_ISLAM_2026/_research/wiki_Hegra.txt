'''Hegra''' ({{Langx|ar|الْحِجْر|translit=al-Ḥijr}}), also known as '''Mada'in Salih'''<ref>{{Cite web|title=Hijr UNESCO World Heritage Site, Mada'in Salih {{!}} ExperienceAlUla.com|url=https://experiencealula.com/en/Discover_AlUla/Heritage/Pages/Hijr.aspx|access-date=3 June 2020|website=experiencealula.com}}{{Dead link|date=December 2021 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> ({{langx|ar|مَدَائِن صَالِح|translit=Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ|lit=Cities of Salih}}), is an [[archaeological site]] located in the area of [[Al-'Ula]]<ref>{{Cite web |title=Mada'in Saleh – Arabian Rock Art Heritage |url=http://saudi-archaeology.com/sites/madain-saleh/ |access-date=3 December 2022 |language=en-US}}</ref> within [[Medina Province (Saudi Arabia)|Medina Province]] in the [[Hejaz]] region, [[Saudi Arabia]].

A majority of the remains date from the time of the [[Nabataean Kingdom]], when the city was founded. The site constituted the kingdom's southernmost and second largest city after its capital, [[Petra]] (modern-day [[Jordan]]).<ref>{{cite news |website=[[BBC News]] |title=Saudi Arabia's silent desert city |first=Marjory |last=Woodfield |date=24 February 2022 |url=http://www.bbc.com/travel/story/20170418-saudi-arabias-silent-desert-city}}</ref> Traces of [[Lihyan]]ite and [[Roman Empire|Roman]] occupation before and after the Nabataean rule, respectively, can also be found. The site features more than 110 well-preserved Nabataean tombs carved into sandstone outcrops.<ref>{{cite web | title=Hegra Archaeological Site (al-Hijr / Madā ͐ in Ṣāliḥ) |website=whc.unesco.org |url= https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1293/ |access-date= 10 April 2025}}</ref>

The archaeological site lies in an arid environment. The dry climate, the lack of resettlement after the site was abandoned, and the prevailing local beliefs about the locality have all led to the extraordinary state of [[Historic preservation|preservation]] of Al-Hijr,<ref name= whs/> providing an extensive picture of the Nabataean lifestyle. Thought to mark the southern extent of the Nabataean kingdom,<ref name=expansion>{{cite web|url=http://www.madainSalih.net/Expansion_of..html|title=History: Expansion of the Nabataeans|work=Historical Madain Salih|access-date=7 April 2014}}{{Dead link|date=April 2024 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> Al-Hijr's oasis agriculture and extant wells exhibit the necessary adaptations made by the Nabataeans in the given environment—its markedly distinct settlement is the second largest among the Nabataean kingdom, complementing that of the more famous Petra archaeological site in Jordan.<ref name=whs/> The location of the site at the crossroads of trade, and the various languages, scripts and artistic styles reflected in the façades of its monumental tombs further set it apart from other archaeological sites. It has duly earned the nickname "The Capital of Monuments" among Saudi Arabia's 4,000 archaeological sites.<ref name=digging/><ref name=madain/>

The [[Quran]]<ref name="Q7:73-79">{{cite quran|7|73|e=79|s=ns|t=p}}</ref><ref name="Q11:61-69">{{cite quran|11|61|e=69|s=ns|t=p}}</ref><ref name="Q15:80-84">{{cite quran|15|80|e=84|s=ns|t=p}}</ref><ref name="Q26:141-158">{{cite quran|26|141|e=158|s=ns|t=p}}</ref><ref name="Q54:23-31">{{cite quran|54|23|e=31|s=ns|t=p}}</ref><ref name="Q89:6-13">{{cite quran|89|6|e=13|s=ns|t=p}}</ref><ref name="Q91:11-15">{{cite quran|91|11|e=15|s=ns|t=p}}</ref> places the settlement of the area by the [[Thamud]]i people during the days of the prophet [[Saleh (prophet)|Salih]], between those of [[Noah in Islam|Nuh (Noah)]] and [[Hud (prophet)|Hud]] on one hand, and those of [[Abraham in Islam|Ibrahim (Abraham)]] and [[Moses in Islam|Musa (Moses)]] on the other. However, a definitive historical chronology can not be obtained through the order of verses because the Quranic chapters (see [[surah]]) deal with different subjects in non-chronologic order.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.alim.org/library/quran/surah/english/17/ASD|title=The Message of the Quran, 1982. [Note] Surah 17:2 briefly discusses Moses, followed by 17:3 dealing with Noah. Then Surah 17:59 deals with the Thamud, 17:61 deals with Adam's creation.|last=Asad|first=M.|access-date=21 December 2019|archive-date=22 December 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191222134656/http://www.alim.org/library/quran/surah/english/17/ASD|url-status=dead}}</ref> According to the Quran, the Thamudis were punished by God for their [[idolatry]], struck by an earthquake and [[lightning]] blasts. Thus, the site has earned a reputation as a [[curse]]d place—an image which the national government is attempting to overcome as it seeks to develop Mada'in Salih for its potential for tourism.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Wood |first=Graeme |date=3 March 2022 |title=Absolute Power |url=https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2022/04/mohammed-bin-salman-saudi-arabia-palace-interview/622822/ |access-date=10 March 2022 |website=The Atlantic |language=en}}</ref>

In 2008, [[UNESCO]] proclaimed Mada'in Salih as a site of patrimony, becoming Saudi Arabia's [[List of World Heritage sites in Saudi Arabia|first World Heritage Site]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=5 July 2022 |title=Buried stories: the tombs of Hegra |url=https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/paid-content-buried-stories-the-tombs-of-hegra |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220701103624/https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/paid-content-buried-stories-the-tombs-of-hegra |url-status=dead |archive-date=1 July 2022 |access-date=3 December 2022 |website=Travel |language=en}}</ref> It was chosen for its well-preserved remains from [[late antiquity]], especially the 131 monumental [[rock-cut tomb]]s, with their elaborately ornamented façades, of the Nabataean Kingdom.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1293|title=Al-Hijr Archaeological Site (Madâin Sâlih)|website=UNESCO World Heritage Centre|language=en|access-date=23 November 2018}}</ref>

== Name ==
Its long history and the multitude of cultures occupying the site have produced several names. References by [[Strabo]] and other Mediterranean writers use the name Hegra ({{langx|grc|{{script|Grek|Ἔγρα}}}}) for the Nabataean site.<ref name="Harrison2016" /><ref>[https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0239%3Abook%3D16%3Achapter%3D4%3Asection%3D24 Strabo, Geography], 16 April 2024</ref><ref>[https://topostext.org/work/241#E260.11 Stephanus of Byzantium, Ethnica, E260.11]</ref> The etymology of the name is not clear, and the Semitic root ''hjr'' can mean many things: "surround, encompass" (Aramaic); "what is defended, prohibited and illicit" or "wall, high wall" (Arabic).{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=288}}

Mada'in Salih, meaning "the cities of Salih", started to be used during the [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] period at the earliest. It refers to the [[Salih]], one of the [[prophet]]s of the [[Quran]] that was unable to convince the Thamudeans to abandon their polytheism.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=288}}
[[File:Susa darius statue subject l07 arab (1).jpg|thumb|The word {{transl|egy-x-late|hgr}} ({{lang|egy-x-late|{{script|Egyp|{{huge|𓉔𓎼𓃭𓈉}}}}}}, Hegra) on an Egyptian-style statue of the Persian king Darius|297x297px]]
Although Hegra was for a long time not as important as [[Al-'Ula|Dedan]], foreigners appear to have called the oasis Hegra. For example, the famous statue of the Achaemenid king [[Darius the Great]] made in Egypt and erected in Susa calls the Arabs {{transl|egy-x-late|hgr}} ({{lang|egy-x-late|{{script|Egyp|{{huge|𓉔𓎼𓃭𓈉}}}}}}).<ref>{{Cite web |title=Hegra – Livius |url=https://www.livius.org/articles/place/dedan/hegra/ |access-date=14 March 2023 |website=www.livius.org}}</ref>

==Location and climate==
The [[archaeology|archaeological]] site of Hegra is situated {{convert|20|km|mi|abbr=on}} north of the town of [[Al-'Ula]],<ref>{{Cite web|title=AlUla the place of heritage for the world|url=https://experiencealula.com/en/Pages/default.aspx|access-date=12 June 2020|website=experiencealula.com|archive-date=11 May 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200511200544/https://experiencealula.com/en/Pages/default.aspx|url-status=dead}}</ref> {{convert|400|km|mile|abbr=on}} northwest of [[Medina]], and {{convert|500|km|mile|abbr=on}} southeast of [[Petra]], Jordan. [[Istakhri]] wrote in his ''Routes of the Realms'' ({{lang|ar|{{script|Arab|مسالك الممالك}}}}):
{{blockquote|Al-Hijr is a small village. It belongs to [[Wadi al-Qura|Wadi al Gura]] and is located at one day's travel inside the mountains. It was the homeland of the Thamudians. I have seen those mountains and their carvings. Their houses are similar to ours but are carved in the mountains, which are called the Ithlib mountains. It looks as if they are a continuous range but they are separated and have sand dunes around them. You can reach the top of the mountains, but this is extremely tiring. The well of the Thamudians which is mentioned in the Holy Quran is located in the middle of the mountains.<ref name=town>{{Cite web |title= Mada'in Salih, a Nabataean town in north west Arabia: analysis and interpretation of the excavation 1986–1990 |url= https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299451129}}</ref>}}

The site is on an inland plain at the foot of [[Jabal al-Uwayrid]], [[basalt]] [[plateau]] that forms a portion of the [[Hijaz Mountains]] in the eastern Hijaz. Beneath the western and northwestern parts of the site, the [[water table]] can be reached at a depth of {{convert|20|m|ft}}.<ref name=whs/> The landscape is characterized by isolated [[Paleozoic|Palaeozoic]] sandstone outcrops shaped by intense erosion during the late Miocene. These sandstones can range from reddish-brown [[Cambrian]] layers to lighter [[Ordovician]] formations; their fine grain made them well suited to rock carving, though their resistance to erosion varies, with some strata heavily affected by wind erosion and salt crystallization.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=290}} The sandstone outcroppings of the desert landscape can vary greatly in size and height.<ref name=hidden/>

The climate is extremely arid, with average annual [[rainfall]] below 50&nbsp;mm. Despite this, the site occupies a favorable [[Hydrology|hydrological]] position within a natural drainage gully that collects runoff from surrounding massifs. This runoff recharged shallow [[groundwater]], which in antiquity lay less than 10 m below the surface. Exploiting these conditions, the Nabataeans developed an [[oasis]] sustained primarily by numerous deep wells rather than by surface channels, enabling [[Irrigation|irrigated]] agriculture and supporting both the local population and travelers through the region.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=290}}

==History==
{{See also|Archaeology of the Arabian Peninsula}}

===Rock writings===
Recent archaeological work has revealed numerous rock writings and pictures not only on Mount Athleb, but also throughout central Arabia.<ref>{{cite encyclopedia |title=Thamūd |encyclopedia=Encyclopædia Britannica |date=21 April 2016 }}</ref> They date between the sixth century BC and the fourth century AD and are labelled as being [[Thamudic]], a name invented by nineteenth-century scholars for these large numbers of inscriptions which had not yet been properly studied.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://krc.orient.ox.ac.uk/ociana/|title=The Online Corpus of the Inscriptions of Ancient North Arabia – Home|last=dan|website=krc.orient.ox.ac.uk|access-date=15 July 2016}}</ref>

===Lihyan/Dedanite era===
Archaeological traces of [[cave art]] on the sandstones and [[Epigraphy|epigraphs]] (inscriptions), considered by experts to be [[Lihyan]]ite script, on top of the Athleb Mountain,<ref name=creation>{{cite web |title=HISTORY: Creation of Al-Hijr |work=Historical Madain Salih |url=http://www.madainSalih.net/Creation_of_Al-Hijr.html |access-date=7 April 2014 }}{{Dead link|date=December 2021 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> near Hegra (Madaʾin Salih), have been dated to the 3rd–2nd century BC,<ref name=whs/> indicating the early human settlement of the area, which has an accessible source of freshwater and fertile soil.<ref name=creation/><ref name=madain>{{cite web |title=History: Madain Salih |work=Historical Madain Salih |url=http://www.madainSalih.net/HISTORY.html |access-date=20 February 2013 }}{{Dead link|date=December 2021 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> The settlement of the [[Lihyan]]s became a centre of commerce, with goods from the east, north and south converging in the locality.<ref name=creation/>

===Nabataean era===
[[File:Madain Saleh (6720497611).jpg|thumb|Multiple Nabataean Tombs in Hegra (Madain Saleh)]]
The extensive settlement of the site took place during the 1st century AD,<ref name="britannica-13-818" /> when it came under the rule of the Nabataean king [[Aretas IV Philopatris]] (Al-Harith IV) (9&nbsp;BC&nbsp;– 40&nbsp;AD), who made Hegra (Madaʾin Salih) the kingdom's second capital, after [[Petra]] in the north.<ref name="creation" /><ref>{{Cite web |title=Hegra: A carved phenomenon envisioning the past |url=https://www.visitsaudi.com/en/see-do/destinations/alula/a-carved-phenomenon-envisioning-the-past |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210421111910/https://www.visitsaudi.com/en/see-do/destinations/alula/a-carved-phenomenon-envisioning-the-past |url-status=dead |archive-date=21 April 2021 |access-date=3 December 2022 |website=www.visitsaudi.com |language=en }}</ref> The place enjoyed a huge urbanization movement, turning it into a city.<ref name="creation" /> Characteristic of Nabateaan rock-cut architecture, the geology of Hegra (Madaʾin Salih) provided the perfect medium for the carving of monumental structures, with [[Nabataean alphabet|Nabataean scripts]] inscribed on their façades.<ref name="whs" />[[File:Myrrh.JPG|thumb|[[Myrrh]] was one of the [[luxury good|luxury items]] that had to pass through the Nabataean territory to be traded elsewhere]]

The Nabataeans also developed [[oasis]] agriculture<ref name=whs/>—digging [[Water well|wells]] and rainwater tanks in the rock and carving places of worship in the sandstone outcroppings.<ref name=madain/> Similar structures were featured in other Nabataean settlements, ranging from southern [[Syria (region)]] to the north, going south to the [[Negev]], and down to the immediate area of the Hejaz.<ref name=whs/> The most prominent and the largest of these is Petra.<ref name=whs/> The well and irrigation system allowed for the cultivation of [[date palms]], [[wheat]], [[barley]], [[olives]], [[pomegranates]], [[figs]], [[lentils]], [[peas]], and [[alfalfa]], and possibly also [[cotton]].<ref>{{cite web |last1=Testard-Vaillant |first1=Philippe |title=Hegra's Splendor Revealed |url=https://news.cnrs.fr/articles/hegras-splendor-revealed |website=CNRS News |publisher=French National Center for Scientific Research (CNRS) |access-date=16 April 2026 |language=en |date=18 May 2018}}</ref>

At the [[Crossroads (culture)|crossroad]] of commerce, the Nabataean kingdom flourished, holding a monopoly for the trade of [[incense]], [[myrrh]] and [[spices]].<ref name=hidden/><ref name=britannica-8-473/> Situated on the overland [[Camel train|caravan]] route and connected to the [[Red Sea]] port of Egra Kome,<ref name= whs/> Hegra, as it was known among the Nabataeans, reached its peak as the major staging post on the main north–south trade route.<ref name= madain/>

===Roman era===
In 106 AD, the Nabataean kingdom was annexed by the contemporary [[Roman Empire]] during the reign of the emperor [[Trajan]].<ref name=britannica-8-473/><ref name=fall>{{cite web |title=History: Fall of Al-Hegra |work=Historical Madain Salih |url=http://www.madainSalih.net/Fall_of_Al-Hijr.html |access-date=7 April 2014 }}{{Dead link|date=December 2021 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref><ref name="romans2" /> Hegra, which is part of the Hejaz, became part of the Roman [[Arabia Petraea|province of Arabia]].<ref name=whs/>

{{Blockquote|The Hedjaz region was integrated into the [[Roman Arabia|Roman province of Arabia]] in 106 AD. A monumental Roman epigraph of 175–177 AD was recently discovered at al-Hijr (then called "Hijr" and now Mada'in Salih).<ref name=whs/>}}
[[File:Hegra Inscription Marcus Aurelius.jpg|thumb|upright=1.4|Hegra Roman inscription dedicated to Emperor [[Marcus Aurelius]], 175–177 AD<ref>{{Cite book|title=Roads of Arabia: Archaeology and History of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia|url=https://books.google.ca/books/about/Roads_of_Arabia.html?id=a9TqzZvHahsC&redir_esc=y|publisher=Somogy Art Publishers|date=2010|isbn=978-2-7572-0395-8|language=en|first=Musée du|last=Louvre|pages=304–305}}</ref>]]
Unfortunately, and unlike other major ex-Nabataean sites like Petra and Bostra, there is no historical information recorded about Hegra in the Roman period. Everything known about the place comes exclusively from [[archaeological excavation]]s. Over 30 [[Pre-Islamic Arabian inscriptions|inscriptions]], written in [[Nabataean script|Nabataean]], [[Greek language|Greek]], and [[Latin]] have been found from this period.{{Sfn|Fiema|2025|p=28–29}} No Nabataean, Greek, or Latin inscriptions are generally found south of Hegra, making it something of a candidate for the southern terminus of how far the Roman occupation of the Arabian Peninsula extended.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=287–288}}

In the Roman era, Hegra continued as a provincial centre, and a 175–177 AD Latin inscription calls it the ''civitas Hegrenorum'', indicating its civic status. Furthermore, it was in this time that that the city saw an influx of traders and artisans, a sizable expansion of its Eastern Mediterranean imports, and a spike in local food production, trends that are partly associated with the Roman troops stationed at Hegra. The main sanctuary was upgraded, and the southeastern gate was re-built in the late second or early third century.{{Sfn|Fiema|2025|p=27–28}}

According to the inscriptions found, the main Roman army unit stationed at Hegra was from the [[Roman legion|legion]] called ''[[Legio III Cyrenaica]]'' (Third Cyrenaican Legion), units of which participated in the original annexation of Nabataea. Soldiers from a few other legions were also stationed in Hegra. The Romans there built a [[citadel]] fortification, and this fort acted as the headquarters of the local Roman troops. The Roman army abandoned Hegra by the late second or early third century, and civilian occupation section of the fort abandoned by the fourth century.{{Sfn|Fiema|2025|p=28–29}} The civilian population of Hegra as a whole persisted into the sixth century at minimum.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=302–303}}

In the early sixth century, Hegra may have re-entered the Roman orbit: [[Justinian I]], to counter the encroachment of the [[Sasanian Empire]], pressed hard to expand his sway over the Arabian Peninsula by establishing a series of client states throughout it. Justinian is said to have been given rule over the "Palm Grove" (either Hegra or [[Tayma]]) by its leader [[Abu Karib ibn Jabalah]], who Justinian in turn made phylarch over the region.{{Sfn|Fisher|2021|p=593–595}}<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Whately |first=Conor |date=2024-12-31 |title=Procopius on the Palm Grove |url=https://journals.akademicka.pl/cc/article/view/6181 |journal=Classica Cracoviensia |language=en |volume=27 |pages=357–374 |doi=10.12797/CC.27.2024.27.14 |issn=2391-6753|doi-access=free }}</ref> In addition, a bowl decorated with a lion has been found at Hegra, with an artistic style typical of the late Roman Byzantine period, most likely the sixth century, suggesting long-distance trade with the Byzantine realm at this time.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=307}}

By the end of the Roman era, Hegra began to lose its status as a centre of trade, leading to its eventual abandonment.<ref name="fall" /> This was because the trading itinerary that once passed through Hegra changed from the overland north–south axis on the [[Arabian Peninsula]] to the maritime route through the Red Sea.<ref name="madain" /> Many experts, based on archaeological studies, believe that the site lost all urban functions by the beginning of [[late antiquity]], mainly due to [[desertification]].<ref name="whs" />

=== Early Islamic period ===
The [[early Islamic period]] of Hegra is very poorly documented. Only a few pottery sherds and Arabic inscriptions are known so far.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=302}}

=== Medieval Islamic era ===
Hegra served as a station along the [[Hajj]] route in the Islamic period, providing supplies and water for pilgrims. The Arab traveller [[Al-Maqdisi]], in the 10th century, noted that the site was a small oasis and activity focused on its wells. Al-Maqdisi is the only account, between the 4th and 19th centuries, to note human activity in the area. The 14th-century traveller [[Ibn Battuta]] noted the red stone-cut tombs of Hegra, by then known as "al-Hijr", but made no mention of human activity.<ref name="whs" />

===Ottoman era===
[[File:Mada'in Salih Ottoman fort.jpg|thumb|The Ottoman Hajj Fort at Mada'in Salih, 1907|176x176px]]

The [[Ottoman Empire]] annexed western Arabia from the [[Mamluk Sultanate|Mamluks]] by 1517.<ref name=britannica-13-820/> In early Ottoman accounts of the Hajj road between [[Damascus]] and Mecca, Hegra (Madaʾin Salih) is not mentioned, until 1672, when the Turkish traveller, [[Evliya Celebi]] noted that the caravan passed through a place called "Abyar Salih" where there were the remains of seven cities.<ref name=Petersen146>Petersen 2012, p. 146.</ref> It is again mentioned by the traveller Murtada ibn 'Alawan as a rest stop on the route called "al-Mada'in".<ref name=Petersen146/> Between 1744 and 1757,<ref name=whs/><ref name=madain/> a fort was built at al-Hijr on the orders of the Ottoman governor of [[Damascus Eyalet|Damascus]], [[As'ad Pasha al-Azm]].<ref name=Petersen146/> A cistern supplied by a large well within the fort was also built, and the site served as a one-day stop for Hajj pilgrims where they could purchase goods such as dates, lemons and oranges.<ref name=Petersen146/> It was part of a series of fortifications built to protect the pilgrimage route to Mecca.<ref name=Petersen146/>

According to the researches of Al-Ansari, the Ottoman castle was found near the settlement dating to the year 1600 AD in 1984.<ref name=town/>

===19th century===
[[File:Hejaz railway.svg|thumb|Map of the [[Hejaz Railway]] that passed through Mada'in Salih|239x239px]]

In 1812, Swiss explorer [[Johann Ludwig Burckhardt]] rediscovered Petra for the Western world. In the aftermath of the news making the rounds, [[Charles Montagu Doughty]], an English traveller, heard of a similar site near Hegra (Madaʾin Salih), a fortified Ottoman town on the Hajj road from Damascus. To access the site, Doughty joined the Hajj caravan, and reached the site of the ruins in 1876, recording the visit in his journal which was published as ''[[Travels in Arabia Deserta]]''.<ref name=madain/><ref name=fall/> Doughty described the Ottoman fort, where he resided for two months, and noted that [[Bedouin]] tribesmen had a permanent encampment just outside of the building.<ref name=Petersen146/>

In the 19th century, there were accounts that the extant wells and oasis agriculture of al-Hijr were being periodically used by settlers from the nearby village of [[Tayma]].<ref name=madain/><ref name=fall/> This continued until the 20th century, when the [[Hejaz Railway]] that passed through the site was constructed (1901–08) on the orders of Ottoman [[Sultan]] [[Abdul Hamid II]] to link [[Damascus]] and [[Jerusalem]] in the north-west with Medina and Mecca,<ref name=madain/><ref name=fall/> hence facilitating the pilgrimage journey to the latter and to politically and economically consolidate the Ottoman administration of the centres of Islamic faith.<ref name=baker/> A station was built north of al-Hijr for the maintenance of [[locomotive]]s, and offices and dormitories for railroad staff.<ref name=madain/> The railway provided greater accessibility to the site. However, this was destroyed in a [[Arab Revolt|local revolt]] during World War I.<ref name=britannica-5-809/> Despite this, several archaeological investigations continued to be conducted in the site beginning in the World War I period to the establishment of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in the 1930s up to the 1960s.<ref name=whs/><ref name=britannica-13-840/> The former railway station, restored and redeveloped, has been transformed into the Chedi Hegra Resort, opened in 2024. The project incorporates the historic station within the hotel complex, designed by the Milan-based architecture firm Giò Forma in collaboration with Black Engineering, and operated by General Hotel Management Ltd. (GHM) under its luxury brand ''The Chedi''.<ref>{{Cite press release |last=AlUla |first=Royal Commission for |title=Royal Commission for AlUla (RCU) partners with luxury hotel operator GHM to develop The Chedi Hegra within the Hegra UNESCO World Heritage Site |url=https://www.prnewswire.com/in/news-releases/royal-commission-for-alula-rcu-partners-with-luxury-hotel-operator-ghm-to-develop-the-chedi-hegra-within-the-hegra-unesco-world-heritage-site-301799032.html |access-date=2 May 2024 |website=www.prnewswire.com |language=en}}</ref>

By the end of the 1960s, the Saudi Arabian government devised a program to introduce a sedentary lifestyle to the [[nomadic]] [[Bedouin]] tribes inhabiting the area.<ref name=whs/> It was proposed that they settle down in al-Hijr, re-using the already existent wells and agricultural features of the site.<ref name=whs/> However, the official identification of al-Hijr as an archaeological site in 1972 led to the resettlement of the Bedouins towards the north, beyond the site boundary.<ref name=whs/> This also included the development of new agricultural land and freshly dug wells, thereby preserving the state of al-Hijr.

===Recent developments===
In 1962, examples of many [[Epigraphy|inscriptions]] were discovered and renewed the archaeological assessment of Hijr (Madaʾin Salih) by Winnett and Reed.<ref name=town/> Although the Al-Hijr site was proclaimed as an archaeological treasure in the early 1970s, few investigations had been conducted since.<ref name=digging/> Healey studied here in 1985 and wrote a book about the inscriptions of Hijr (Madaʾin Salih) in 1993.<ref name=town/>

[[Wahhabism#Destruction of Islam's early historical sites|The prohibition on the veneration of objects/artifacts]] has resulted in minimal archaeological activities. These conservative measures started to ease up beginning in 2000, when Saudi Arabia invited expeditions to carry out archaeological explorations as part of the government's push to promote cultural heritage protection and tourism.<ref name=digging/><ref name=hidden/> The archaeological site was proclaimed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008.<ref name=unesco/> More recent archaeological studies of the area have been made as part of efforts to document and preserve the heritage sites prior to opening the area to more tourism.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Heritage Sites in AlUla, Saudi Arabia {{!}} ExperienceAlUla.com|url=https://experiencealula.com/en/Discover_AlUla/Heritage/Pages/default.aspx|access-date=23 June 2020|website=experiencealula.com|archive-date=3 June 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200603043452/https://experiencealula.com/en/Discover_AlUla/Heritage/Pages/default.aspx|url-status=dead}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Keith |first=Lauren |title=Hegra, an Ancient City in Saudi Arabia Untouched for Millennia, Makes Its Public Debut |url=https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/hegra-ancient-city-saudi-arabia-untouched-for-millennia-makes-its-public-debut-180976361/ |access-date=3 December 2022 |website=[[Smithsonian Magazine|Smithsonian]] |language=en}}</ref>

Non-monumental burial sites, totaling 2,000, are also part of the place.<ref name=whs/> A closer observation of the façades indicates the [[social status]] of the buried person<ref name=madain/>—the size and ornamentation of the structure reflect the wealth of the person. Some façades had plates on top of the entrances providing information about the grave owners, the religious system, and the masons who carved them.<ref name=hidden/> Many graves indicate military ranks, leading archaeologists to speculate that the site might once have been a Nabataean military base, meant to protect the settlement's trading activities.<ref name=creation/>

The Nabataean kingdom was not just situated at the crossroad of trade but also of culture. This is reflected in the varying motifs of the façade decorations, borrowing stylistic elements from [[Assyria]], [[Phoenicia]], [[Ancient Egypt|Egypt]] and [[Hellenistic period|Hellenistic]] [[Alexandria]], combined with the native artistic style.<ref name= whs/> Roman decorations and [[Latin alphabet|Latin scripts]] also figured on the troglodytic tombs when the territory was annexed by the Roman Empire.<ref name=digging/> In contrast to the elaborate exteriors, the interiors of the rock-cut structures are severe and plain.<ref name=hidden/>

A religious area, known as "''Jabal Ithlib''," is located to the north-east of the site.<ref name=whs/> It is believed to have been originally dedicated to the Nabataean deity [[Dushara]]. A narrow corridor, {{convert|40|m|ft|0}} long between the high rocks and reminiscent of the [[Siq]] in Petra, leads to the hall of the ''Diwan'', a Muslim's council-chamber or law-court.<ref name=whs/> Small religious sanctuaries bearing inscriptions were also cut into the rock in the vicinity.

The residential area is located in the middle of the plain, far from the outcrops.<ref name=whs/> The primary material of construction for the houses and the enclosing wall was sun-dried [[mudbrick]].<ref name=whs/> Few vestiges of the residential area remain.

Water is supplied by 130 wells, situated in the western and north-western part of the site, where the water table was at a depth of only {{convert|20|m|ft|abbr=on}}.<ref name=whs/> The wells, with diameters ranging {{convert|4|–|7|m|ft|abbr=on}}, were cut into the rock, although some, dug in loose ground, had to be reinforced with sandstone.<ref name=whs/>

==In the Qur'an==
According to the Quran, the site of al-Hijr was settled by the tribe of Thamud,<ref name=creation/> who "(took) for (themselves) palaces from its plains and (carved) from the mountains, homes".{{cite quran|7|73–79}}{{cite quran|11|61–69}}{{cite quran|15|80–84}} The tribe fell to idol worship and [[oppression]] became prevalent.<ref name=verses>{{cite web |title=History: Explanation of the Verses |work=Historical Madain Salih |url=http://www.madainSalih.net/Explanation_of__Verses.html |access-date=7 April 2014 }}{{Dead link|date=December 2021 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref> The prophet Salih,<ref name="Q7:73-79"/><ref name="Q11:61-69"/><ref name="Q26:141-158"/><ref name="Q54:23-31"/><ref name="Q89:6-13"/><ref name="Q91:11-15"/> to whom the site's name of ''Mada'in Salih'' is often attributed,<ref name=madain/> called on the Thamudis to repent.<ref name=verses/> The Thamudis disregarded the warning and instead commanded Salih to summon a pregnant [[she-camel of God]] from the back of a mountain, and a pregnant she-camel was sent to the people from the back of the mountain as proof of Salih's divine mission.<ref name=verses/><ref name=thamud/>

However, only a minority heeded his words. The non-believers killed the sacred camel instead of caring for it, and its calf ran back to the mountain from whence it came. The Thamudis were given three days before their punishment was to take place, since they disbelieved and did not heed the warning. Salih and his [[tawhid|monotheistic]] followers left the city, but the others were punished by [[God in Islam|God]]—their souls leaving their lifeless bodies in the midst of an earthquake and lightning blasts.<ref name=hidden/><ref name=verses/>

[[Robert G. Hoyland]] suggested that their name was subsequently adopted by other new groups that inhabited the region of Mada'in Salih after the disappearance of the original people of Thamud.<ref>{{cite book|last=Hoyland|first=Robert G.|title=Arabia and the Arabs: From the Bronze Age to the Coming of Islam|year=2001|publisher=Routledge|isbn=0415195349|page=69|url={{Google books|lZ8LydOFoScC|Arabia and the Arabs: From the Bronze Age to the Coming of Islam|page=69|plainurl=yes}}}}</ref> This suggestion is also supported by the narration of [[Abd Allah ibn Umar ibn al-Khattab]] and the analysis of [[ibn Kathir]], which report that people called the region of Thamud ''al-Hijr'', while they called the province of Mada'in Salih as ''Arḍ Thamūd'' 'Land of Thamud' and ''Bayt Thamud'' 'House of Thamud'.<ref>[[Sahih al-Bukhari]], Narrated: ʿAbdullah ibn ʿUmar, Hadiths: 2116 & 3379</ref><ref>{{cite book|last1=Ibn Kathir|author-link=Ibn Kathir|title=Al-Bidâya wa-l-Nihâya ("The Beginning and the End") Vol.&nbsp;1|date=2003|publisher=Dar al-Kutub al-Ilmiyya|location=Beirut|page=159}}</ref> The term "Thamud" was thus not applied to the groups that lived in Mada'in Salih, such as [[Lihyan]]ites and [[Nabataeans]],<ref>The New Encyclopædia Britannica: Macropædia Volume 13. USA: Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc. 1995. Page: 818</ref><ref>Encyclopædia Britannica, Under the Category of: History of Arabia, the Section of: Dedān and Al-Ḥijr</ref> but rather to the region itself, and according to classical sources, it was agreed upon that the only remaining Thamudites were the [[Banu Thaqif]], who inhabited the city of [[Taif]] south of [[Mecca]].<ref>Jawwad Ali, ''The Detailed History of Arabs before Islam'', Volume&nbsp;15, p.&nbsp;301.</ref><ref>''The Historical Record of Ibn Khaldon'', Volume&nbsp;2, p.&nbsp;641.</ref><ref>Kitab Al-Aghani, Abu Al-Faraj Al-Asfahani, Volume&nbsp;4, p.&nbsp;74.</ref>

== Tombs ==
Funerary monuments are the most prominent and best-preserved remains at Hegra. As at Petra, [[rock-cut tomb]]s dominate the archaeological landscape, but at Hegra they are often preserved in better condition. The funerary record includes three main types: monumental rock-cut tombs, simple trench graves, and tumuli, reflecting social hierarchy and long-term use of the site.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=291–296}}

The [[monumental tomb]]s are carved into the [[sandstone]] outcrops surrounding the urban centre and were deliberately oriented toward the city to maximize visibility and commemorate elite families. These tombs form several necropolises encircling the settlement. In contrast, trench graves, cut directly into the bedrock, are found on the summits of the crags and were intended for non-elite individuals; thousands have been documented. [[Tumulus|Tumuli]], consisting of stone mounds, are mainly located on plateaus west of the city. Excavation of one tumulus revealed a tower-tomb structure that may predate the Nabataean period but was reused between the first century BC and early third century AD.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=291–296}}

Eighty-six monumental tombs display carved facades belonging to a distinct [[Nabataean architecture|Nabataean architectural]] tradition. Several stylistic groups have been identified, including early "proto-Hegra" types and a later "Hegra type," distinguished by the presence of an attic. Tomb size varies greatly, from modest façades to monumental examples over twenty metres high, the largest of which is called al-Farid.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=291–296}}

Unlike most tombs at Petra, many Hegra tombs bear Nabataean inscriptions carved above the entrance. These functioned as legal documents recording ownership, burial rights, prohibitions, and dates based on the reigns of Nabataean kings. The tomb owners belonged to the urban elite and included both men and women, as well as officials and professionals. Internally, tombs were collective family burial chambers containing multiple graves cut into walls or floors and added over time, reflecting prolonged and repeated use across generations.{{Sfn|Nehme|Al-Talhi|Villeneuve|2010|p=291–296}}
