The '''Zamzam Well''' ({{langx|ar|بئر زمزم|translit=Biʾru Zamzam}} {{IPA|ar|biʔru zam.zam}}) is a [[Water well|well]] located within the [[Masjid al-Haram]] in [[Mecca]], Saudi Arabia. It is located {{convert|20|m|abbr=on}} east of the [[Kaaba]],<ref name="survey">{{cite web |title=Zamzam Studies and Research Centre |url=http://www.sgs.org.sa/english/earth/pages/zamzam.aspx |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130619152954/http://www.sgs.org.sa/english/earth/pages/zamzam.aspx |archive-date=June 19, 2013 |work=Saudi Geological Survey |language=ar}}</ref> the holiest place in [[Islam]].
[[File:Puits de Zamzam avant les modifications.jpg|thumb|A person extracting water from the well prior to the installation of a pump]]
In Islamic narratives, the well is a miraculously generated source of water, which opened up thousands of years ago when [[Ishmael in Islam|Ismaʿil]] ([[Ishmael]]), the son of [[Abraham in Islam|Ibrahim]] ([[Abraham]]), was left with his mother [[Hagar in Islam|Hajar]] ([[Hagar]]) in the desert. It is said to have dried up or been buried  while the tribe [[Jurhum]] lived in the area. The well is claimed to have been rediscovered and excavated in the 6th century by [[Abd al-Muttalib]], grandfather of the Islamic prophet [[Muhammad]].

== Etymology ==
The origin of the name is uncertain.<ref name=":1">{{Cite book |last=Hughes |first=Thomas Patrick |title=Dictionary of Islam : Being a Cyclopaedia of the Doctrines, Rites, Ceremonies, and Customs, Together With the Technical and Theological Terms, of the Muhammadan Religion |year=1885 |isbn=978-0-243-60987-1 |pages=701 |publisher=Forgotten Books |oclc=1152284802}}</ref> According to historian [[Jacqueline Chabbi]], the noun {{langx|ar|زمزم|translit=Zamzam}} is an [[onomatopoeia]]. She associates the noun with the adjectives {{langx|ar|زمزم|translit=zamzam}} and {{langx|ar|زمازم|translit=zumāzim}} which are onomatopoeic denoting a dull sound stemming from either a distant roll (of thunder) or a guttural sound emitted with a closed mouth by animals or people, however meaning either 'an abundant supply of water' or 'a source of water which does not dry up' if applied to {{Langx|ar|ماء|translit=māʾ|lit=water}}. She states that this latter meaning of an unintelligible guttural sound encompasses a layer of meaning associated with the sacred and mystical, in addition to the basic notion of the sound possibly being related to the concept of an abundant flow of water.

Early Islamic sources use the terms {{langx|ar|زمزم|translit=zamzama}} and {{langx|ar|زمازم|translit=zamāzima}} to refer to the religious rites of [[Zoroastrianism]] and the Zoroastrians. The terms are onomatopoeic and derive from what Arabs perceived to be an indistinct, droning sound of the recitation of [[Avestan]] [[Avesta|prayers and scriptures]] by [[Magi]].{{Efn|"'''ZAMZAMA''' <small>(A.)</small>, in early Arabic "the confused noise of distant thunder" (Lane, 1249b), but widely used in the sources for early Islamic history for the priests of the Magians reciting and intoning the Zoroastrian prayers and scriptures, producing (to the Arabs' ears) an indistinct, droning sound. Thus in [[al-Ṭabarī]], i, 1042, we have the ''zamzama'' of the Herbadhs, in 2874 the ''muzamzim'' or adherent of Zoroastrianism, and in 2880 ''zamzama'' for the Zoroastrian rites and ''zamāzima'' for the Magians in general."<ref>{{Cite book |title=The encyclopaedia of Islam  |date=2002 |publisher=Brill |editor= Peri Bearman |editor2=Thierry Bianquis |editor3=C. Edmund Bosworth |editor4=E.J. van Donzel |editor5=Wolfhart Heinrichs |editor6=H. A. R. Gibb |isbn=90-04-16121-X |edition= |volume=XI |location=Leiden |pages=442 |oclc=399624}}</ref>}}

Medieval Arabic writers like [[Al-Masudi|al-Masʿūdī]] generally state the well is named on the account of {{langx|ar|زمزم|translit=zamzama|lit=prayers recited by Zoroastrians}}. They argue that based on their "kinship with [[Abraham]]" Zoroastrians regularly made pilgrimages to Mecca to pray over the well. A later account by [[Badr al-Din al-Ayni|al-ʿAynī]] claims that the well is named after {{langx|ar|زمازم|translit=zamāzima}} supposedly meaning 'bridles' which had been donated to the well by and named after [[Sasan]], the Zoroastrian progenitor of the [[Sasanian Empire]].

Other medieval Arabic sources connect the name to the [[Angel Gabriel in Islam|Angel Gabriel]] being the source of the murmuring being captured by this onomatopoeia.<ref name=":2">{{Cite book |last=Chabbi |first=Jacqueline |title=The encyclopaedia of Islam. |date=2002 |publisher=Brill|editor= Peri Bearman |editor2=Thierry Bianquis |editor3=C. Edmund Bosworth |editor4=E.J. van Donzel |editor5=Wolfhart Heinrichs |editor6=H. A. R. Gibb|isbn=90-04-16121-X |edition= |volume=XI |location=Leiden |pages=440–441 |oclc=399624}}</ref>

[[Thomas Hughes (priest, born 1838)|Hughes]] additionally identifies a tradition of deriving the name from an exclamation supposedly made by Hajar either being {{Langx|ar|زم، زم!|translit=zamm, zamm!|lit=fill, fill!}}  or supposedly [[Egyptian language|Egyptian]] meaning "Stop, stop!".{{Efn|[[George Sale]] speaks of this claim in an introductory essay to his translation of the Quran. He however states that it was most likely named after the "murmuring of its waters".}}<ref name=":1" /><ref>{{Cite book |last=George |first=Sale |url=https://quran-archive.org/explorer/george-sale/1734?page=138#top |title=The Koran: commonly called the Alcoran of Mohammad |date=1734 |publisher=F. Warne and Co |edition=1st |location=London |pages=118 |oclc=61585803 }}{{Dead link|date=July 2025 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref>

== Traditional accounts ==

=== Traditional origins ===
[[File:Khalili Collection Hajj and Arts of Pilgrimage mxd 0366.jpg|thumb|right|An imperial pilgrim's water flask from [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Turkey]], used to collect water from the Zamzam Well, bearing the Sultan's [[tughra]]]]
Islamic tradition states that the Zamzam Well was opened up in some form by God to assist [[Hagar in Islam|Hajar]], the second wife of [[Abraham in Islam|Ibrahim]] and mother of [[Ishmael in Islam|Ismaʿil]]. In Islamic narratives Ibrahim, commanded by God, led Hajar and Ismaʿil to the area of present-day Mecca, there he left them alone in the desert. The two are narrated to have been suffering severely from thirst. In some versions of the story Hajar walks back and forth between the two hills of [[Safa and Marwa]]h in search of water.{{Efn|Some records state this to have occurred seven times. Muslim pilgrims during Hajj similarly walk seven-times between these two hills, an act ritual anámnēsis stemming from a belief in the traditional account of the well's appearance.<ref>{{Citation |chapter=Miracles and Religion |date=2018-11-16 |chapter-url=https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780748699070-003/html |title=Islam, Christianity and the Realms of the Miraculous |pages=22 |publisher=Edinburgh University Press |doi=10.1515/9780748699070-003 |isbn=978-0-7486-9907-0 |access-date=2022-10-11|chapter-url-access=subscription }}</ref>}} The story of the appearance of the well either involves the infant Isma'il scraping the ground with his feet and water springing out or God sending [[Gabriel]] ([[Holy Spirit (Islam)|Jibra'il]]) who consequently opens up the well using a variety of methods depending on the narration.<ref name=islamonline>{{cite web | author = Mahmoud Ismael Shil and 'Abdur-Rahman 'Abdul-Wahid | title = Historic Places: The Well of Zamzam | url = http://www.islamonline.net/English/hajj/2002/01/stories/article4.shtml | access-date =August 6, 2008 | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20080223204039/http://www.islamonline.net/English/hajj/2002/01/stories/article4.shtml| archive-date = February 23, 2008}}</ref><ref name=":2" /><ref name=":4">{{Cite journal |last=Hawting |first=G. R. |date=1980 |title=The Disappearance and Rediscovery of Zamzam and the 'Well of the Ka'ba' |journal=Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies, University of London |volume=43 |issue=1 |pages=44–54 |doi=10.1017/S0041977X00110523 |jstor=616125 |s2cid=162654756 |issn=0041-977X}}</ref> The wealth of mystic discourses discussing the history of the well is from the [[Abbasid-era|Abbasid era]] and largely extra-Quranic, as the well is not referred to directly by the Quran.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Chabbi |first=Jacqueline |title=The encyclopaedia of Islam. |date=2002 |publisher=Brill |isbn=90-04-16121-X |editor= Peri Bearman |editor2=Thierry Bianquis |editor3=C. Edmund Bosworth |editor4=E.J. van Donzel |editor5=Wolfhart Heinrichs |editor6=H. A. R. Gibb |edition= |volume=XI |location=Leiden |pages=441 |oclc=399624}}</ref>

According to Islamic tradition, Ibrahim rebuilt a shrine called {{Langx|ar|بيت ٱللَّٰه|translit=Baytu 'l-llah|lit=House of God}} near the site of the well. A building supposedly first constructed by [[Adam]], which Muslim tradition regards as the origin of the [[Kaaba|Kaʿba]].{{Efn|Presently the well is located approximately {{convert|20|m|abbr=on}} east of the Kaaba.<ref name=survey/>}} The well is meant to have dried up (possibly as punishment) during the settlement of the tribe of the [[Jurhum]], who initially are meant to have migrated to the area from Yemen. In some narrations focused on the objects deposited in Zamzam the well simply dries up, then prior to the Jurhum being forced to leave Mecca because of God expelling them for their misdeeds their leader buries sacred objects from the Kaʿba in the location of Zamzam. In others, focused on the well itself, the objects are placed in Zamzam itself, with the well ultimately being buried by the leader of the Jurhum.<ref name=":4" /><ref name=":3">{{Cite book |last=Chabbi |first=Jacqueline |title=The encyclopaedia of Islam. |date=2002 |publisher=Brill |editor= Peri Bearman |editor2=Thierry Bianquis |editor3=C. Edmund Bosworth |editor4=E.J. van Donzel |editor5=Wolfhart Heinrichs |editor6=H. A. R. Gibb |isbn=90-04-16121-X |edition= |volume=XI |location=Leiden |pages=442 |oclc=399624}}</ref>

=== Traditional rediscovery ===
According to traditional Muslim accounts, [[Abd al-Muttalib|ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib]] is responsible for the rediscovery of the well. He purportedly had a divine [[precognition]] about the well at some point in his life. A common narrative relayed by [[Ibn Ishaq]] via [[Ibn Hisham]] involves four dreams, the first three concern mysterious objects called {{Langx|ar|طيبة|translit=Ṭayba}}, {{Langx|ar|برة|translit=Barra}} and {{Langx|ar|المضنونة|translit=al-Maḍnūna}} he is meant to dig for, the fourth then names Zamzam. Other accounts omit the third dream naming {{Langx|ar|المضنونة|translit=al-Maḍnūna}}.{{Efn|Hawting's analysis indicates that the fourth dream naming Zamzam may be a late addition to this oral tradition.}} Following ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib's precognitions to dig, he is claimed to have found a number of artefacts: golden gazelle figurines, armour, and several specimens of a type of sword called {{Langx|ar|السيف القلعى|translit=as-Sayf al-Qalaʿī|lit=Qalaʿī sword}}. [[Ibn Sa'd|Ibn Saʿd]] relays two separate traditions regarding the discovery of a well by ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, one involving Zamzam's discovery and water disputes among the [[Quraysh]], the other involving the series of dreams and said artefacts but neither water nor the name Zamzam.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Hawting |first=G. R. |date=1980 |title=The Disappearance and Rediscovery of Zamzam and the 'Well of the Ka'ba' |journal=Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies, University of London |volume=43 |issue=1 |pages=44–47 |doi=10.1017/S0041977X00110523 |jstor=616125 |s2cid=162654756 |issn=0041-977X}}</ref><ref name=":3" />

Hawting's analysis argues that the oral traditions surrounding the {{langx|ar|بئر الكعبة|translit=Biʾru 'l-Kaʿba|lit=Well of the Kaʿba}}, a dry well inside the Kaʿba reputed to have been used as the treasury of the Kaʿba and as a place for religious offerings in pre-Islamic Mecca and Zamzam merged at some point with stories concerning the former being adapted to feature the latter.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Hawting |first=G. R. |date=1980 |title=The Disappearance and Rediscovery of Zamzam and the 'Well of the Ka'ba' |journal=Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies, University of London |volume=43 |issue=1 |pages=52–54 |doi=10.1017/S0041977X00110523 |jstor=616125 |s2cid=162654756 |issn=0041-977X}}</ref>

=== Subsequent history ===
Commentators of the Abbasid-era mystic discourses surrounding the well, like al-Masʿūdī expanded on these extra-Quranic accounts. They further connect the site to Zoroastrianism via what they interpret to be Zoroastrian religious artefacts uncovered by ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib in combination with their etymological analyses. Ultimately arguing that due to the well's history supposedly being related to (what they perceive to have been) the true religion of the [[Iranian peoples|peoples of Iran]], the [[Islamization of Iran|Islamisation of Iran]] is to be understood and framed as a rediscovery.<ref name=":3" />

The traditional Islamic account of the well's history relayed by [[Al-Azraqi|al-Azraqī]] traces the lineage of its management via to [[Abu Talib ibn Abd al-Muttalib|Abū Tālib]] as inherited from its (re)-discoverer and his father [[Abd al-Muttalib|ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib]]. The usage rights to the well were then supposedly sold due to Abū Tālib's financial troubles to his brother, [[Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib|al-ʿAbbās]], who is meant to have instituted the first new ordinance concerning the well by banning all non-religious body-hygienic uses of it. Thus the management of the Zamzam Well (a responsibility called {{Langx|ar|سقاية|translit=siqāya|lit=giving of water}}) was reputedly a hereditary position of the [[Abbasid dynasty|Abbasids]], i.e. the progeny of al-ʿAbbās, who ended up seizing control over Mecca as a whole using their [[Abbasid Caliphate|caliphate]].<ref>{{Cite book |last=al-Azraqī |first=Abū l-Walīd Ahmad ibn Muhammad |url=https://archive.org/details/diechronikender00wsgoog/page/n256/mode/2up?view=theater |title=Aḫbār Makka wa-mā ǧāʾa fī-hā min al-āṯār |year=1858 |editor-last=Wüstenfeld |location=Leipzig |publisher=F. A. Brockhaus |pages=297 |language=ar |script-title=ar: أخبار مكة وما جاء فيها من الأثار |trans-title=Reports of Mecca and Ones which Concern her Monuments}}</ref> Buildings called the {{Langx|ar|بيت الشراب |translit=Baytu 'š-Šarrāb|lit=House of Drinking}}, variously referred to also as the {{Langx|ar|قبات الشراب|lit=Domes of Drinking|translit=Qubbātu 'š-Šarāb}}, were structures used to store jars of Zamzam water away from the heat. One of which is claimed to have been initially built on orders from and named after [[Abbas ibn Ali|Abbas]], founder of the Abbasid dynasty himself.{{Efn|The other having been called the Dome of the Jews.}}<ref name=":6">{{Cite book |last=Porter |first=Venetia |title=Hajj : global interactions through pilgrimage |date=2015 |editor=Luitgard E. M. Mols |editor2=Marjo Buitelaar |isbn=978-90-8890-286-4 |location=Leiden |publisher=Sidestone Press |pages=97 |chapter=Gifts, Souvenirs, and the Hajj |oclc=904132495}}</ref> However, al-Azraqī describes the well being fully in the open during the age of [[Ibn Abbas|ʿAbdallāh]], son of ʿAbbās. He reports it being surrounded by two pools: one pool between the well and a corner of the Kaʿba, used for quenching the thirst of pilgrims; and another pool behind the well used for [[Wudu|wuḍūʾ]], i.e. religious ritual cleaning, with a subterranean drainage channel leading the waste water out of the Masjid al-Haram's courtyard. He describes the well's operators pouring the water drawn alternately between the pools using [[waterskin]]s.<ref>{{Cite book |last=al-Azraqī |first=Abū l-Walīd Ahmad ibn Muhammad |url=https://archive.org/details/FP17219/02_17219/page/n58/mode/2up |title=Aḫbār Makka wa-mā ǧāʾa fī-hā min al-āṯār |publisher=Dār al-Andalus |year=1983 |editor-last=Malḥas |editor-first=Rušdī aṣ-Ṣāliḥ |volume=II |location=Beirut |pages=58–59 |language=ar |script-title=ar: أخبار مكة وما جاء فيها من الأثار |trans-title=Reports of Mecca and Ones which Concern her Monuments}}</ref> In the years 775–778 the second Abbasid caliph, [[al-Mansur]], sponsored extensive construction projects in the Masjid al-Haram, which included paving the area around Zamzam with [[marble]].<ref name=":5">{{Cite book |last=Peters |first=F. E. |title=Mecca : a Literary History of the Muslim Holy Land. |date=1994 |publisher=Princeton University Press |isbn=978-1-4008-8736-1 |location=Princeton |pages=402 |oclc=978697983}}</ref> Beginning in the years 833–855, during the rule of [[Al-Mu'tasim|al-Muʿtasim]], a civil servant called ʿUmar ibn Faraǧ ar-Ruḫḫaǧī began a series of construction projects related to the well, all marked by their intricate use of [[mosaic]]s. He ended up constructing a dome covering the well and demolishing a previous structure surrounding it.{{Efn|Possibly having simply been a light wall made of stones according to some sources}} Additionally a dome connecting the new dome over the well and the House of Drinking was constructed. Ar-Ruḫḫaǧī had the House of Drinking demolished in 843–844, during the rule of [[al-Wathiq]], and went on to have three small domes surrounding the dome covering Zamzam built in its stead.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Leal |first=Bea |date=2020-10-02 |title=The Abbasid Mosaic Tradition and the Great Mosque of Damascus |journal=Muqarnas Online |volume=37 |issue=1 |pages=32–33 |doi=10.1163/22118993-00371p03 |s2cid=230662012 |issn=0732-2992|url=https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3032266a-7e9f-4082-a87b-12d8fff5bae1 }}</ref><ref name=":5" />

In the year 930 a minority branch of [[Isma'ilism|Ismaʿilism]] called the [[Qarmatians]] launched an [[Sack of Mecca|attack on Mecca]]. They had previously attacked many caravans of the Abbasid Caliphate, including those of pilgrims travelling to the city. They would seize the [[Black Stone]] and move it to the capital of their own state in [[Eastern Arabia|Bahrayn]] for 22 years. They were briefly led in 931 by a Persian, [[Abu'l-Fadl al-Isfahani|al-Isfahani]], whom they believed to be God incarnate who unsuccessfully attempted to convert the Qarmatian state to Zoroastrianism. They destroyed the dome covering Zamzam. It is relayed by historians such as {{Interlanguage link|Qutb ad-Dīn an-Nahrawālī|lt=Qutb ad-Dīn|de||WD=}} that in the aftermath of their attack on the city, the Well Zamzam, as well as all other wells in the city were filled with corpses of pilgrims.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Peters |first=F. E. |title=Mecca : a Literary History of the Muslim Holy Land. |date=1994 |publisher=Princeton University Press |isbn=978-1-4008-8736-1 |location=Princeton |pages=123–124 |oclc=978697983}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Foundation |first=Encyclopaedia Iranica |title=Welcome to Encyclopaedia Iranica |url=https://iranicaonline.org/ |access-date=2022-10-11 |website=iranicaonline.org |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Citation |last=Schick |first=Rober |chapter=The Umayyads and ʿAbbāsids in Mujīr al-Dīn's Fifteenth-Century History of Jerusalem and Hebron |date=2012-01-01 |chapter-url=https://brill.com/view/book/edcoll/9789004231948/B9789004231948_011.xml |title=The Lineaments of Islam |pages=209–232 |editor-last=Cobb |editor-first=Paul |publisher=BRILL |doi=10.1163/9789004231948_011 |isbn=978-90-04-21885-7 |access-date=2022-10-11}}</ref>
[[File:Adriaan-Reland-Verhandeling-van-de-godsdienst-der-Mahometaanen MG 0723.tif|thumb|The domed building covering Zamzam which was destroyed in 1803 (D) and Zamzamis in front of the Domes of Drinking (P) {{circa}} 1718]]
Following the Abbasid relinquishment of the management of the well a quasi-guild called Zamzamis emerged and remained in place at least until the end of the 19th century. While theoretically anyone could go to the well and fill a vessel they brought at the well, in practice this group of specialised men ended up being primarily responsible for distributing the well's water. They did so by storing two kinds of filled jugs, some on wooden podiums with metal cups attached others hidden in the shade. They performed this service in theory without demanding payment. In 19th century practice however a pilgrim would be assigned a Zamzami upon his arrival and would have been obliged to pay him a small initial sum of $1 for a jug to bear his name. This jug would then be kept by the Zamzami for the pilgrim. Payment was in practice also expected for the further services of the Zamzami. These included bringing the pilgrim water throughout his pilgrimage and pouring it over his body for purposes of ritual cleaning ([[Wudu|wuḍūʾ]]) among others. To nudge the pilgrim into donating adequately the Zamzami would show the pilgrim the bad state of the prayer mats he laid out in the Masjid for the pilgrims and explain his dire need for donations. The more generous the pilgrim's donations were, the more expansive the Zamzami's services became; with services such as procuring bottled Zamzam water to be taken home after the pilgrimage and bringing jugs right to the pilgrim's abode in Mecca being reserved for the most generous. To be allowed into the trade of being a Zamzami a costly license from the [[Sharif of Mecca|Grand Sharif]] was required. During the reign of [[Awn ar-Rafiq]] as Grand Sharif for example such a license cost £50.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Peters |first=F. E. |title=Mecca : a Literary History of the Muslim Holy Land. |date=1994 |publisher=Princeton University Press |isbn=978-1-4008-8736-1 |location=Princeton |pages=282–283 |oclc=978697983}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Stratkötter |first=Rita |title=Von Kairo nach Mekka |date=2021 |publisher=Klaus Schwarz Verlag |isbn=978-3-11-240097-5 |location=Berlin |pages=109 |language=de |chapter=Ausgewählte Aspekte der Pilgerfahrt |oclc=1286881241}}</ref>
[[File:Khalili Collection Hajj and Arts of Pilgrimage pot 1955.jpg|left|thumb|16th century depiction of the Domes of Drinking and the containers set up all over the masjid by the Zamzamis]]
In the 15th century the [[Mamluk]] Sultan [[Qaitbay]] invested into improving the well's water quality, as well as funding the construction of a new dome covering the well.<ref name=":7">{{Cite journal |last=Burak |first=Guy |date=2017-10-08 |title=Between Istanbul and Gujarat: Descriptions of Mecca in the Sixteenth-Century Indian Ocean |journal=Muqarnas Online |volume=34 |issue=1 |pages=290, 311, 314 |doi=10.1163/22118993_03401p012 |issn=0732-2992}}</ref> In 1489, during the reign of [[List of Mamluk sultans|Mamluk Sultan]] Malik an-Nāṣir, reconstruction of the Dome of Abbas, i.e. one of the two Domes of Drinking, occurred. There had been structures bearing this name and function again at least since 1183.<ref name=":6" /> The new structure featured a large painted gate made from yellow stone, a large fountain in its middle, three iron windows, and two metal fountains for pilgrims to drink from all housed under a large dome.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Burak |first=Guy |date=2017-10-08 |title=Between Istanbul and Gujarat: Descriptions of Mecca in the Sixteenth-Century Indian Ocean |journal=Muqarnas Online |volume=34 |issue=1 |pages=303 |doi=10.1163/22118993_03401p012 |issn=0732-2992}}</ref> Following the Ottoman conquest of the Mamluk Sultanate and their assumption of power in Mecca, [[Suleiman the Magnificent]] funded many construction and renovation works in the city. One of these began in 1540 with the destruction of the roof covering the well, which had remained intact since Qaitbay's rule. While construction was disrupted by a flood, completion of the new roof occurred in January 1542.<ref name=":7" /> In 1621 the [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] Sultan [[Ahmed I]] had an iron cage constructed around the well.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Dördüncü |first= Mehmet Bahadir |title=Mecca – Medina : the Yıldız albums of Sultan Abdülhamid II |date=2006 |publisher=The Light |isbn=1-59784-054-8 |pages=21 |oclc=836279213}}</ref> In 1660 Ottoman authorities constructed a new building over Zamzam.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Peters |first=F. E. |title=Mecca : a Literary History of the Muslim Holy Land. |date=1994 |publisher=Princeton University Press |isbn=978-1-4008-8736-1 |location=Princeton |pages=406 |oclc=978697983}}</ref> Following their conquest of Mecca in 1803, during the reign of [[Selim III]], the [[Wahhabism|Wahhabis]], led by [[Abdulaziz bin Muhammad Al Saud]], destroyed the dome covering the well.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Hathaway |first=Jane |title=The Arab lands under Ottoman rule, 1516–1800 |date=2008 |publisher=Routledge |isbn=978-0-582-41899-8 |location=Harlow, England |pages=219 |oclc=175284089}}</ref> [[File:1888 Ka'aba and Zamzam.png|thumb|Building covering Zamzam (3) in 1888]]
In 1964 the last building covering the well of Zamzam, which had been rebuilt by Ottoman authorities following the destruction of the building housing Zamzam by the [[Emirate of Diriyah|first Saudi State]], was demolished by authorities of the [[third Saudi State]] (known at that time already as the [[Saudi Arabia|Kingdom of Saudi Arabia]]). In its place the opening of the well was moved to a basement, {{convert|2.5|m|ft}} deep, to free up space above ground for pilgrims.<ref name=":8">{{Cite web |date=2022-04-07 |title=How Saudi authorities overseeing the holy Zamzam well have moved with the times |url=https://arab.news/b2ugg |access-date=2022-10-14 |website=Arab News |language=en}}</ref>

== Technical information ==
[[File:Wadi Ibrahim & Zamzam Well.svg|thumb|Wadi Ibrahim & Zamzam Well hydrogeological schematic]]

The well of Zamzam was expanded by hand over the years, and is about {{cvt|30|m|-1}} deep and {{convert|1.08|to|2.66|m|abbr=on}} in diameter. It taps groundwater from the [[Wadi Ibrahim]] [[alluvium]] and from [[bedrock]].

Water formerly was drawn from the well with ropes and buckets; since 1964, the well itself opens into a basement room inaccessible to the public, where it can be seen behind glass panels. Two electric pumps, operating alternately, move the water {{convert|5|km|mi|abbr=on}} southwards at a pace between 11 and 18.5 litres (2½ and 4½ gallons) per second to the King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz Zamzam Water Project in Kudai. The center opened in September 2010 and cost 700 million [[Saudi riyal]]s to construct; it is operated by the National Water Company of Saudi Arabia. In this location the water is treated, using filters and ultraviolet light, stored, and distributed.

Once treated, the water is stored in one of two reservoirs: the first, located in Kudai, can hold 10,000 cubic meters (13,000 cu. yd.) of water; the other, King Abdulaziz Sabeel Reservoir in [[Medina]], has a capacity of 16,000 cubic meters (20,000 cu. yd.). The Kudai reservoir is connected by pipes to drinking fountains in the Masjid al-Haram. The Medina reservoir supplies the [[Al-Masjid an-Nabawi|Prophet's Mosque]]. Additionally, unbottled water is distributed using tanker trucks which transport 150,000 litres (35,000 gal.) per day at normal times and up to 400,000 litres per day (100,000 gal.) during pilgrimage seasons to the Medina location. Unbottled water is available through the before-mentioned drinking fountains, a fountain meant for pilgrims wishing to fill larger containers not intended for immediate consumption, and sterilised containers placed by authorities throughout the holy sites in Mecca and Medina. These latter containers come in several variants, chilled and unchilled, as well as being either stationary or worn as a backpack by employees of the complexes with disposable plastic cups provided in any case. Small filtered water bottles are also distributed free of charge at the holy sites. The water distributed this way in the Masjid al-Haram totals ca. 700,000 litres (175,000 gal.) per day outside of pilgrimage season and 2,000,000 litres (500,000 gal.) per day during said season. Distribution outside the Islamic holy sites within the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia occurs with the water being bottled in 10-litre (2½ gallon) containers which are sold directly at a warehouse at the site of the King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz Zamzam Water Project or via [[hypermarket]]s and [[Big-box store|super stores]] throughout the state. In 2018 the number of 10-litre (2½ gallon) containers distributed per day was 1.5 million.<ref name=":8" /><ref>{{Citation |last=General Presidency of the Holy Mosque and the Prophet's Mosque |title=Documentary Film "Zamzam Blessed Water" |date=April 16, 2018 |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZL3MJb_djUU |language=en |access-date=2022-10-14}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |author=Staff Writer |last2=Gazette |first2=Saudi |title=King Abdullah Project in Makkah to resume Zamzam distribution from March 23 |url=https://www.zawya.com/en/business/king-abdullah-project-in-makkah-to-resume-zamzam-distribution-from-march-23-byfl0h74 |access-date=2022-10-14 |website=www.zawya.com |language=en}}</ref> In 2010 the annual limit on how much water can be extracted from the well was stated as {{circa}} 500,000 cubic meters (700,000 cu. yd) per year, though due to annual variations in rainfall patterns there exists a lot of deviation with regards to how much water can be extracted without lowering the well's water level too drastically each year.<ref name="survey" />

[[Hydrogeology|Hydrogeologically]], the well is in the ''Wadi Ibrahim'' (Valley of Abraham). The upper half of the well is in the sandy alluvium of the valley, lined with stone masonry except for the top metre (3&nbsp;ft) which has a concrete "collar". The lower half is in the bedrock. Between the alluvium and the bedrock is a {{convert|1/2|m|adj=on}} section of permeable weathered rock, lined with stone, and it is this section that provides the main water entry into the well. Water in the well comes from absorbed rainfall in the Wadi Ibrahim, as well as run-off from the local hills. Since the area has become more and more settled, water from absorbed rainfall on the Wadi Ibrahim has decreased.

The [[Saudi Geological Survey]] has a "Zamzam Studies and Research Centre" which analyses the technical properties of the well in detail. Water levels were monitored by [[hydrograph]], which in more recent times has changed to a digital monitoring system that tracks the water level, electric conductivity, [[pH]], [[Reduction potential|Eh]], and temperature. All of this information is made continuously available via the Internet. Other wells throughout the valley have also been established, some with digital recorders, to monitor the response of the local [[aquifer]] system.<ref name=survey/>

Zamzam water is colourless and odorless, but has a distinctive taste, with a pH of 7.9–8, and so is slightly alkaline.<ref>{{cite journal |url= https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51712613 |title=Water quality of bottled water in the kingdom of Saudi Arabia: A comparative study with Riyadh municipal and Zamzam water |journal=Journal of Environmental Science and Health, Part A |volume=46 |publisher=[[Taylor & Francis]] |issue=13 |pages=1519–1528 |date=October 12, 2011 |doi=10.1080/10934529.2011.609109|pmid=21992118 |last1 = Alfadul|first1 = Sulaiman M.|last2=Khan |first2=Mujahid A. |bibcode=2011JESHA..46.1519A |s2cid=21396145 }}</ref>

==Safety of Zamzam water==
According to the SGS, the Zamzam Well is tested on a daily basis, in a process which involves the taking of three samples from the well. These samples are then examined at the King Abdullah Zamzam Water Distribution Center in Mecca, which is equipped with advanced facilities.<ref name="Arabnews">{{Cite web |date=2011-05-08 |title=Kingdom rejects BBC claim of Zamzam water contamination |url=https://www.arabnews.com/node/376786 |access-date=2022-09-23 |website=Arab News |language=en}}</ref>

The Zamzam well was renovated in 2018 by the [[Saudi Arabia|Saudi]] authorities. The project involved cleaning of the areas around the Zamzam well by removing the debris of concrete and steel used in the old cellar of the [[Masjid al-Haram|Grand Mosque]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.dhakatribune.com/world/middle-east/2017/10/30/zamzam-well-renovation|title=Sacred Zamzam well to go under renovation|date=2017-10-30|newspaper=[[Dhaka Tribune]]|access-date=2020-04-22}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=http://saudigazette.com.sa/article/527572/SAUDI-ARABIA/Zamzam-project-to-be-ready-before-Ramadan|title=Zamzam project to be ready before Ramadan|date=2018-02-03|website=Saudigazette|language=en|access-date=2020-04-22}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.arabnews.com/node/1185376/saudi-arabia|title=Zamzam well to be renovated before Ramadan|date=2017-10-30|newspaper=[[Arab News]]|language=en|access-date=2020-04-22}}</ref><ref name=":0">{{Cite web|url=http://saudigazette.com.sa/article/535205/SAUDI-ARABIA/100-samples-of-Zamzam-water-tested-everyday|title=100 samples of Zamzam water tested everyday|date=2018-05-19|website=Saudigazette|language=en|access-date=2020-04-22}}</ref> During [[Ramadan]], 100 samples are tested every day to ensure the water's good quality.<ref name=":0" />

=== Historical controversy ===
Cholera epidemics and the Hajj had become an issue of debate since an 1866 International Sanitary Conference in [[Istanbul]]. The conference however identified British [[Steamship|steam-ships]] transporting [[Islam in India|Indian Muslims]] to the Hajj as mainly responsible for the globalisation of [[cholera]]. The Ottoman state consequently instituted a [[quarantine]] system using the [[Red Sea]] to protect public health. Britain tried to undermine this system in the coming decades fearing public backlash in India and restrictions on its ability to engage in [[free trade]].

The first controversy surrounding the safety of water from the Zamzam Well began in 1883. In 1881, James Zohrab, British Consul in [[Jeddah]] sent samples of water, which he alleged to be Zamzam water, to [[Edward Frankland]], who published his findings in 1883. Frankland claimed the water from the well to be six times more contaminated by animal waste than [[sewage]] in [[London]]. Arguing that due to human waste being simply buried in the ground in Mecca, the groundwater had become highly contaminated and a source of cholera. This situation, coupled with the city's visitors from all around the [[Muslim world]], was thought to have spread the disease effectively throughout it. He ultimately called for the closure of Zamzam as a public health measure to protect the people of Asia and Europe.

Frankland's report became the basis for a treatise presented to the Ottoman Board of Health by the Dutch emissary thereto. The treatise was received very negatively and interpreted as anti-Islamic propaganda. Mehmed Şakir Bey, an [[Epidemiology|epidemiologist]], was so outraged that he consulted [[Charles Bonkowski|Bonkowski Paşa]], the Sultan's head chemist, and Ahmet [[Effendi|Efendi]], professor of chemistry at the [[Ottoman Military College|Ottoman War College]], for further scientific inquiry. Their testing revealed nothing dangerous about the water of Zamzam. By comparing their results to the results Frankland had published they began to publicly doubt the authenticity of his sample, as his sample was almost as saline as sea water. Additionally they explained that the water in Mecca's aqueducts and that from Zamzam came from entirely different sources, that pilgrims did not so much as bathe near the Masjid al-Haram, and that Zamzam could not be a cholera source because Mecca did not see annual cholera epidemics during the Hajj.

Related to their doubts of the water's authenticity they began to speculate that Yusuf Kudzi, a British protected person and associate of Zohrab, had been the source of the water and had contaminated the water to embarrass the Muslim world. Kudzi was of Russian-Jewish ancestry, had been born in [[Jerusalem]] and was a convert to Islam. The Ottoman establishment of that time had grown to doubt the authenticity of any Muslim cooperating with the British, conceptualising them as not truly Muslim and possibly as being spies.

Britain's intellectuals ended up rejecting [[Robert Koch]]'s (presently widely accepted) findings of cholera being caused by bacteria. They kept arguing that cholera must be caused by local factors and in a manner which cannot be mediated via quarantine. British scientists would continue publishing anti-Zamzam and anti-contagionist articles up to 1895. Their anti-contagionist views would fall further and further away from scientific consensus in the following decades. Concurrently Ottoman authorities continued to invest into persistent and successful efforts seeking to maintain and improve water quality throughout Arabia.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Low |first=Michael Christopher |date=2015 |title=Ottoman Infrastructures of the Saudi Hydro-State: The Technopolitics of Pilgrimage and Potable Water in the Hijaz |journal=Comparative Studies in Society and History |volume=57 |issue=4 |pages=942–946 |doi=10.1017/s0010417515000407 |s2cid=148128135 |issn=0010-4175}}</ref>

===BBC allegation and responses===
In May 2011, a [[BBC London]] investigation alleged that water taken from taps connected to the Zamzam Well contained high levels of [[nitrate]] and [[arsenic]] at levels three times the legal limit in the UK, the same levels found in illegal water purchased in the UK.<ref name=Lynn>{{cite news |title=Contaminated Zamzam holy water from Mecca sold in the UK |first=Guy |last=Lynn |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-london-13267205 |publisher=[[BBC News]] |date=May 5, 2011 |access-date=May 6, 2011}}</ref>

====Saudi Arabian authorities' response====
The Saudi authorities rejected the BBC's claim and said that the water is fit for human consumption. An official from the Saudi Arabian embassy in London stated 'water from the Zamzam well is not contaminated and is fit for human consumption. Genuine Zamzam water does not contain arsenic'.<ref>{{cite news| date= 8 May 2011| url= https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-london-13326566 |title= 'No arsenic in genuine holy water', Saudis say| work= BBC News| access-date= 8 May 2011}}</ref> The president of the Saudi Geological Survey (SGS), Zuhair Nawab, has stated that the Zamzam Well is tested on a daily basis, in a process involving the taking of three samples from the well.<ref name="Arabnews"/>

The BBC article concentrated on bottled water supplied by individuals rather than the Presidency of the Two Holy Mosques' Affairs, according to Fahd Turkistani, advisor to the [[General Authority of Meteorology and Environmental Protection]]. Turkistani stated that the Zamzam water pollution may have been caused by unsterilized containers used by illegal workers selling Zamzam water at Makkah gates.<ref name="Arabnews"/>

====Council of British Hajjis====
In the same month that the BBC report was released, the Council of British Hajjis later declared that drinking Zamzam water was safe, contradicting the BBC report. The council noted that the [[Politics of Saudi Arabia|Government of Saudi Arabia]] does not allow the export of Zamzam water for resale. They also stated that it was unknown whether the water being sold in the UK was genuine and that people should not buy it and should report the sellers to the [[Trading Standards]] if they saw it for sale.<ref>{{cite press release|url= http://www.medicalnewstoday.com/releases/225253.php | website= Medical News Today| title= Zam Zam Water Is Safe, UK| date= 13 May 2011 | access-date= 13 July 2015 | publisher= Council of British Hajjis (Pilgrims)}}</ref>
