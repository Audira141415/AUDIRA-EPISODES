'''Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam'''{{efn|{{langx|ar|عَبْدُ اللَّهِ ٱبْن الزُّبَيْرِ ٱبْن الْعَوَّامِ|translit=ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām}}}} (May 624{{Snd}}October/November 692) was the leader of a [[caliphate]] based in [[Mecca]] in opposition to the [[Umayyad Caliphate|Umayyads]] during the [[Second Fitna]] from 683 until his death in 692. 

The son of [[Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]] and [[Asma bint Abi Bakr]], and grandson of the first [[Rashidun]] [[Caliphate|caliph]] [[Abu Bakr]], Ibn al-Zubayr belonged to the [[Quraysh]], the leading tribe of the nascent Muslim community, and was the first child born to the [[Muhajirun]], [[Islam]]'s earliest converts. As a youth, he participated in the [[early Muslim conquests]] alongside his father in [[Muslim conquest of Syria|Syria]] and [[Arab conquest of Egypt|Egypt]], and later played a role in the Muslim conquests of [[Ifriqiyah|North Africa]] and northern Iran in 647 and 650, respectively. During the [[First Fitna]], he fought on the side of his aunt [[Aisha]] against the fourth Rashidun caliph [[Ali]] ({{reign|656|661}}). Though little is heard of Ibn al-Zubayr during the subsequent reign of the first Umayyad caliph [[Mu'awiya I]] ({{reign|661|680}}), it was known that he opposed the latter's designation of his son, [[Yazid I]], as his successor. Ibn al-Zubayr, along with many of the Quraysh and the [[Ansar (Islam)|Ansar]] of Medina, the leading Muslim groups of the [[Hejaz]] (western Arabia), opposed the caliphate becoming an inheritable institution of the [[Umayyad dynasty]].

Ibn al-Zubayr established himself in Mecca where he rallied opposition to Yazid ({{reign|680|683}}), before proclaiming himself caliph in the wake of Yazid's death in 683. Meanwhile, Yazid's son and successor [[Mu'awiya II]] died weeks into his reign, precipitating the collapse of Umayyad authority across the Caliphate, most of whose provinces subsequently accepted the suzerainty of Ibn al-Zubayr. Though widely recognized as caliph, his authority was largely nominal outside of the Hejaz. By 685, the Umayyad Caliphate had been [[Battle of Marj Rahit (684)|reconstituted]] under [[Marwan I]] in Syria and Egypt, while Ibn al-Zubayr's authority was being challenged in Iraq and Arabia by [[Alids|Pro-Alid]] and [[Kharijites|Kharijite]] forces. Ibn al-Zubayr's brother [[Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr|Mus'ab]] [[Battles of Madhar and Harura|reasserted]] Ibn al-Zubayr's suzerainty in Iraq by 687, but was [[Battle of Maskin|defeated]] and killed by Marwan's successor [[Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan|Abd al-Malik]] in 691. The Umayyad commander [[al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf]] proceeded to [[Siege of Mecca (692)|besiege]] Ibn al-Zubayr in his Meccan stronghold, where he was ultimately defeated and killed in 692.

Through the prestige of his family ties and social links with the Islamic prophet [[Muhammad]] and his strong association with the holy city of Mecca, Ibn al-Zubayr was able to lead the influential, disaffected Muslim factions opposed to Umayyad rule. He sought to re-establish the Hejaz as the political center of the Caliphate. However, his refusal to leave Mecca precluded him from exercising power in the more populous provinces where he depended on his brother Mus'ab and other loyal lieutenants, who ruled with virtual independence. He thus played a minor active role in the struggle carried out in his name.

==Early life and career==
===Family===
Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr was born in [[Medina]] in the [[Hejaz]] (western Arabia) in May 624.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}} He was the eldest son of [[Zubayr ibn al-Awwam]], a [[companion of Muhammad]] and a leading Muslim figure.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}}{{sfn|Hasson|2002|p=549}} He belonged to the [[Asad ibn Abd-al-Uzza|Banu Asad]] clan of the [[Quraysh]],{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}}{{sfn|Hasson|2002|p=549}} the dominant tribe of [[Mecca]], a trade center in the Hejaz and location of the [[Kaaba]], the holiest sanctuary in Islam. Ibn al-Zubayr's paternal grandmother was [[Safiyya bint Abd al-Muttalib]], the paternal aunt of Muhammad,{{sfn|Hasson|2002|p=549}} and his mother was [[Asma bint Abi Bakr]], a daughter of the first Rashidun [[caliph]], [[Abu Bakr]] ({{reign|632|634}}), and sister of [[Aisha]], a wife of Muhammad.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}} According to the ninth-century historians [[Muhammad ibn Habib al-Baghdadi|Ibn Habib]] and [[Ibn Qutaybah|Ibn Qutayba]], Ibn al-Zubayr was the first child born to the [[Muhajirun]], the earliest converts to Islam who had been exiled from Mecca to Medina.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}} These early social, kinship and religious links to Muhammad, his family and the first Muslims all boosted Ibn al-Zubayr's reputation in adulthood.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}}

Ibn al-Zubayr had a number of wives and children. His first wife was Tumadir bint Manzur ibn Zabban ibn Sayyar ibn Amr of the [[Banu Fazara]].{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=335}}{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=85}} She gave birth to his eldest son Khubayb, hence Ibn al-Zubayr's {{transliteration|ar|[[Kunya (Arabic)|kunya]]}} (epithet) "Abu Khubayb", and other sons Hamza, [[Abbad ibn Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr|Abbad]], al-Zubayr and Thabit.{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=335}}{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=85}} She or another of Ibn al-Zubayr's wives, Umm al-Hasan Nafisa, a daughter of [[Hasan ibn Ali|Hasan]], son of the fourth caliph [[Ali]] ({{reign|656|661}}) and grandson of Muhammad, bore his daughter Ruqayya.{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=335}}{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=147}} Tumadir's sister Zajla was at one point married to Ibn al-Zubayr.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=85, note 404}} He was also married to A'isha, a daughter of the third Rashidun caliph [[Uthman]] ({{reign|644|656}}).{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=335}} A'isha or Nafisa mothered Ibn al-Zubayr's son Bakr,{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=335}} of whom little is reported in the traditional sources.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=115}} Ibn al-Zubayr divorced A'isha following the birth of their son.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=115}} From another wife, Hantama bint Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Harith ibn Hisham, Ibn al-Zubayr had his son Amir.{{sfn|Fishbein|1997|p=159, note 676}}

===Military career===
As a child, during the reign of the second Rashidun caliph [[Umar]] ({{reign|634|644}}) in 636, Ibn al-Zubayr may have been present with his father at the [[Battle of the Yarmuk]] against the [[Byzantine Empire|Byzantine]]s in [[Diocese of the East|Syria]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}} He was also present with his father in [[Amr ibn al-As]]'s [[Arab conquest of Egypt|conquest of Byzantine Egypt]] in 640.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}} In 647, Abd Allah Ibn al-Zubayr distinguished himself in the Muslim conquest of [[Ifriqiya]] (North Africa) under the commander [[Abd Allah ibn Sa'd]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}} During [[Muslim conquest of the Maghreb#The Battle of Sufetula|that campaign]], Ibn al-Zubayr discovered a vulnerable point in the battle lines of the Byzantine defenders and slew their [[Patrician (post-Roman Europe)|patrician]], [[Gregory the Patrician|Gregory]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}}{{sfn|Madelung|1997|p=105}} He was lauded by Caliph Uthman and issued a victory speech, well known for its eloquence, upon his return to Medina.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Madelung|1997|p=105}} Later, he joined [[Sa'id ibn al-As]] in the latter's offensive in northern Iran in 650.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

Uthman appointed Ibn al-Zubayr to the commission tasked with the recension of the [[Qur'an]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} During the rebel siege of Uthman's house in June 656, the caliph placed Ibn al-Zubayr in charge of the defense; the latter was reportedly wounded in the fighting.{{sfn|Madelung|1997|pp=106, 133}} In the aftermath of [[Assassination of Uthman|Uthman's assassination]], Abd Allah fought alongside his father and his aunt Aisha against the partisans of Uthman's successor, the fourth Rashidun caliph Ali ibn Abi Talib, at the [[Battle of the Camel]] in [[Basra]] in December.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} Zubayr ibn al-Awwam was killed, while Ibn al-Zubayr was wounded sparring with one of Ali's commanders, [[Malik al-Ashtar]].{{sfn|Madelung|1997|p=172}} Ali was victorious and Ibn al-Zubayr returned with Aisha to Medina, later taking part in the arbitration to end the [[First Fitna]] in [[Udhruh|Adhruh]] or [[Dumat al-Jandal]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} During the talks, he counseled [[Abd Allah ibn Umar ibn al-Khattab|Abd Allah ibn Umar]] to pay for the support of Amr ibn al-As.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

==Revolt==
===Opposition to the Umayyads===
[[File:Kaba.jpg|thumb|right|300px|The [[Kaaba]] in 1882. Throughout his revolt, Ibn al-Zubayr used the sanctuary as his base of operations and it was twice besieged, in 683 and 692. He rebuilt it following severe damage during the first siege, but his changes were later reversed.]]

Ibn al-Zubayr did not oppose [[Mu'awiya I]]'s accession to the caliphate in 661 and remained largely inactive during the course of his reign.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} However, he refused to recognize Mu'awiya's nomination of his son [[Yazid I]] as his successor in 676.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} When Yazid acceded following his father's death in 680, Ibn al-Zubayr again rejected his legitimacy, despite Yazid having the backing of the Arab tribesmen of [[Bilad al-Sham|Syria]] who formed the core of the Umayyad military.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=46}} In response, Yazid charged [[al-Walid ibn Utba ibn Abi Sufyan]], the governor of Medina, with gaining Ibn al-Zubayr's submission,{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|pp=145–146}} but he evaded the authorities and escaped to Mecca.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} He was joined there by Ali's son [[Husayn ibn Ali|Husayn]], who too had refused submission to Yazid. Husayn and his supporters made a stand against the Umayyads in [[Karbala]] in 680, but Husayn and most of his relatives and companions were killed [[Battle of Karbala|in the battle there]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

Following Husayn's death, Ibn al-Zubayr began clandestinely recruiting supporters.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} By September 683, he had taken control of Mecca.{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=12}} He referred to himself as {{Transliteration|ar|al-ʿaʾidh biʾl bayt}} (the fugitive at the sanctuary, ''viz.'', the Kaaba), adopted the slogan {{transliteration|ar|la hukma illa li-llah}} (judgement belongs to God alone), but made no claim to the caliphate.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=47}}{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|pp=65–66}} Yazid ordered the governor of Medina, [[Al-Ashdaq|Amr ibn Sa'id ibn al-As]], to arrest Ibn al-Zubayr.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=95, note 469}} In turn, the governor instructed Ibn al-Zubayr's estranged brother, the head of Medina's {{transliteration|ar|[[shurta]]}} (security forces), [[Amr ibn al-Zubayr]], to lead the expedition.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|p=95, note 469}} However, the Umayyad force was ambushed and Amr was captured and subsequently killed while in captivity.{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|p=151}} Ibn al-Zubayr declared the illegitimacy of Yazid's caliphate and allied himself with the [[Ansar (Islam)|Ansar]] of Medina, led by [[Abd Allah ibn Hanzala]], who had withdrawn support for Yazid due to his supposed improprieties.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} Ibn al-Zubayr also gained the support of the [[Khawarij|Kharijite]] movement in Basra and [[Eastern Arabia|Bahrayn]] (eastern Arabia);{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=47}} the Kharijites were early opponents of the Umayyads who had defected from Caliph Ali because of his participation in the 657 arbitration.

In response to growing opposition throughout Arabia, Yazid dispatched a Syrian Arab expeditionary force led by [[Muslim ibn Uqba]] to suppress Ibn al-Zubayr and the Ansar.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=47}} The Ansar were routed at the [[Battle of al-Harra]] in the summer of 683, and Ibn Hanzala was slain.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|pp=65–66}}{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} The army continued toward Mecca, but Ibn Uqba died en route and command passed to his deputy [[Husayn ibn Numayr al-Sakuni]].{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} The latter [[Siege of Mecca (683)|besieged the city]] on 24 September after Abd Allah Ibn al-Zubayr refused to surrender.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}}{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} The Kaaba was severely damaged during al-Sakuni's bombardment.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} During the siege, two potential Qurashi candidates for the caliphate, Mus'ab ibn Abd al-Rahman and [[al-Miswar ibn Makhrama]], were killed or died of natural causes.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|pp=65–66}} In November, news of Yazid's death prompted al-Sakuni to negotiate with Ibn al-Zubayr.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} Al-Sakuni proposed to recognize him as caliph on the condition that he would rule from [[Bilad al-Sham|Syria]], the center of the Umayyad military and administration.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} Ibn al-Zubayr rejected this and the army withdrew to Syria, leaving him in control of Mecca.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

===Claim to the caliphate===
[[File:Approximate map of areas under Ibn al-Zubayr's control after the death of Muawiya II.png|thumb|upright=1.75|Map of the Caliphate {{circa|684}}, during the [[Second Muslim Civil War]]. Ibn al-Zubayr's sovereignty as caliph was recognized in the [[Hejaz]], [[Yemen]], [[Egypt in the Middle Ages|Egypt]], [[Iraq]] and the districts of [[Fars province|Fars]] and [[Kerman province|Kerman]] (areas shaded in green)]]

Yazid's death and the subsequent withdrawal of the Umayyad army from the Hejaz afforded Ibn al-Zubayr the opportunity to realize his aspirations for the caliphate.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=47}} He immediately declared himself {{Transliteration|ar|[[amir al-mu'minin]]}} (commander of the faithful), a title traditionally reserved for the caliph, and called for all Muslims to give him their oaths of allegiance.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|pp=65–66}} With the other potential Hejazi candidates dead, Ibn al-Zubayr remained the last contender for the caliphate among the anti-Umayyad factions in Mecca and Medina and most of these groups recognized him as their leader.{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|pp=65–66}} An exception were the [[Banu Hashim]] clan to which Muhammad and the [[Alids]] belonged and whose support Ibn al-Zubayr deemed important for his own legitimacy as caliph.{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=12–13, 21}} The leading representatives of the clan in the Hejaz, [[Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya]], the half-brother of Husayn ibn Ali, and their cousin [[Ibn Abbas|Abd Allah ibn Abbas]], withheld their oaths citing the need for a stronger consensus in the wider Muslim community.{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=12–13, 21}} Irritated, Ibn al-Zubayr besieged the clan's neighborhood in Mecca and imprisoned Ibn al-Hanafiyya to pressure the Banu Hashim.{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=12–13, 21}} Meanwhile, the Kharijites under [[Najda ibn Amir al-Hanafi]] in the [[al-Yamama|Yamama]] (central Arabia) abandoned Ibn al-Zubayr once he forwarded his claim to the caliphate, an institution they rejected, and Ibn al-Zubayr refused to embrace their doctrine.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Ahmed|2010|pp=65–66}}{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}}

In the Umayyad capital [[Damascus]], Yazid was succeeded by his young son [[Mu'awiya II]], but Mu'awiya II wielded virtually no authority and died from illness only months after his accession.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=47}} This left a leadership void in [[Bilad al-Sham|Syria]] as there were no suitable successors among Mu'awiya I's Sufyanid house.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=47}} In the ensuing chaos, Umayyad authority collapsed across the caliphate and Ibn al-Zubayr gained wide recognition.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} Most of the Islamic provinces offered their allegiance, including [[Egypt in the Middle Ages#Umayyad period|Egypt]], [[Kufa]], [[Greater Yemen|Yemen]] and the [[Qays]]i tribes of [[Jund Qinnasrin|northern Syria]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} Likewise, in [[Greater Khorasan|Khurasan]], the ''de facto'' governor [[Abd Allah ibn Khazim al-Sulami]] offered his recognition.{{sfn|Zakeri|1995|p=230}} Ibn al-Zubayr appointed his brother [[Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr|Mus'ab]] as governor of Basra and its dependencies.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} In a testament to the extent of Ibn al-Zubayr's sovereignty, coins were minted in his name as far as the districts of [[Kerman province|Kerman]] and [[Fars province|Fars]] in modern-day Iran; both were dependencies of Basra at that time.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} Nonetheless, his authority outside of the Hejaz was largely nominal.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

Most of the Arab tribes in [[Jund Dimashq|central]] and [[Jund al-Urdunn|southern Syria]], which were dominated by the [[Banu Kalb]], remained loyal to the Umayyads and selected [[Marwan I|Marwan ibn al-Hakam]] from the Abu al-As branch of the Umayyad clan to succeed Mu'awiya II.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} The proclamation of Marwan as caliph in Damascus marked a turning point for Ibn al-Zubayr.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=48}} Marwan's [[Yaman (tribal group)|Yamani]] forces, led by [[Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad]], decisively defeated the Zubayrid loyalist Qaysi tribes, led by [[al-Dahhak ibn Qays al-Fihri]], at the [[Battle of Marj Rahit (684)|Battle of Marj Rahit]] in July 684.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} The surviving Qaysi tribesmen fled to the [[Al-Jazira (caliphal province)|Jazira]] (Upper Mesopotamia) under the leadership of [[Zufar ibn al-Harith al-Kilabi]], who maintained his recognition of Ibn al-Zubayr's suzerainty.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=81}} However, in March 685, Ibn al-Zubayr lost the economically important province of Egypt to Marwan.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|pp=80–81}}

Meanwhile, negotiations collapsed between Ibn al-Zubayr and the Kufan revolutionary [[Mukhtar al-Thaqafi]], who took up the cause of the [[Alids|Alid]] family.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=82}} He declared Ibn al-Hanafiyya the [[Imamate in Shia doctrine|Imam]] and, unprecedented in Islamic history, the [[Mahdi]].{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=82}} Al-Mukhtar's partisans drove out the Zubayrid authorities from Kufa in October 685.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=12–13, 21}}{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=82}} Al-Mukhtar later dispatched a Kufan force to the Hejaz and freed Ibn al-Hanafiyya.{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=12–13, 21}} Mus'ab's authority in Basra and Khurasan was also beginning to waver but was ultimately secured after he gained the backing of the powerful [[Azd]]i chieftain and military leader of Khurasan, [[al-Muhallab ibn Abi Sufra]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} Mus'ab also gained the defections of thousands of Kufan tribesmen and together they defeated and killed al-Mukhtar in April 687.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=83}}{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=21}} Ibn al-Zubayr subsequently dismissed Mus'ab from office in 686/87 and appointed his own son Hamza as governor of Basra.{{sfn|Fishbein|1990|p=118, note 424}} The latter dispatched a force under Abd Allah ibn Umayr al-Laythi to drive out the Najdiyya Kharijites from Bahrayn after they overran the province, but the Zubayrids were repulsed.{{sfn|Fishbein|1990|p=119, note 431}} Hamza proved incompetent in his administration of Iraq and, following his failure to deliver the provincial revenues to the state treasury in Mecca, he was dismissed and allegedly imprisoned by his father.{{sfn|Fishbein|1990|p=118, note 424}}{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=8}} Mus'ab was reinstated shortly after, in 687/688.{{sfn|Fishbein|1990|p=118, note 424}}{{sfn|Anthony|2016|p=8}} By that time, the Najdiyya Kharijites conquered Yemen and [[Hadhramaut]], while in 689, they occupied [[Taif|Ta'if]], Mecca's southern neighbour.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

===Suppression and death===
The defeat of al-Mukhtar, who had opposed the Zubayrids and the Umayyads, left Ibn al-Zubayr and Marwan's son and successor [[Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan|Abd al-Malik]] ({{reign|685|705}}) as the two main contenders for the caliphate.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=83}} However, [[Kharijite]] gains in Arabia had isolated Ibn al-Zubayr in the Hejaz, cutting him off from loyalists in other parts of the caliphate.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} In 691, Abd al-Malik secured the support of Zufar and the [[Qays]] of Jazira, removing the principal obstacle between his Syrian army and Zubayrid Iraq.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=84}} Later that year, his forces conquered Iraq and killed Mus'ab in the [[Battle of Maskin]].{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=84}} Al-Muhallab, who was leading the fight against the Kharijites in Fars and [[Ahvaz|Ahwaz]], subsequently switched his allegiance to Abd al-Malik.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=84}}

After asserting Umayyad authority in Iraq, Abd al-Malik dispatched one of his commanders, [[al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf]], to subdue Ibn al-Zubayr.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} Al-Hajjaj [[Siege of Mecca (692)|besieged Mecca]] for six months, by which point, most of Ibn al-Zubayr's partisans and his sons Khubayb and Hamza surrendered upon offers of pardons.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Fishbein|1990|p=226}} Ibn al-Zubayr remained defiant and acting on his mother's counsel, entered the battlefield where he was ultimately slain by [[al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf]] in 692.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=54}}{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

In an anecdote recorded by 9th-century historian [[al-Tabari]], when al-Hajjaj and his lieutenant commander, [[Tariq ibn Amr]], stood over Ibn al-Zubayr's body, Tariq said of the latter: "Women have borne none manlier than he. He had no defensive trench, no fortress, no stronghold; yet he held his own against us an equal, and even got the better of us whenever we met with him".{{sfn|Peters|1994|pp=100–101}} Al-Hajjaj posted Ibn al-Zubayr's body on a [[Gibbeting|gibbet]] where it remained until Abd al-Malik allowed Ibn al-Zubayr's mother to retrieve it.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} His body was subsequently buried in the house of his paternal grandmother Safiyya in Medina.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} The death of Ibn al-Zubayr and the victory of the Umayyads marked the end of the Second Fitna.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}}

== Descendants ==
Following his victory, Abd al-Malik confiscated the estates of Ibn al-Zubayr in Medina and elsewhere in the Hejaz.{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=331}} The caliph later restored some of the properties to Ibn al-Zubayr's sons after a request by Thabit.{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=331}} His eldest son, Khubayb, was flogged to death in Medina by its governor [[Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz|Umar II]] during the reign of Caliph [[al-Walid I]] ({{reign|705|715}}).{{sfn|Hawting|1989|p=65, note 306}} Thabit, meanwhile, had gained particular favor from al-Walid's successor, Caliph [[Sulayman ibn Abd al-Malik]] ({{reign|715|717}}), who agreed to return the remainder of the confiscated estates to Ibn al-Zubayr's sons.{{sfn|Elad|2016|p=332}} Under the [[Abbasid Caliphate|Abbasid]] caliphs [[al-Mahdi]] ({{reign|775|785}}) and [[Harun al-Rashid]] ({{reign|786|809}}), several descendants of Ibn al-Zubayr attained senior administrative posts, including his great-grandson [[Abd Allah ibn Mus'ab]] and the latter's son [[Bakkar ibn Abd Allah]], who successively served as [[List of caliphal governors of Medina|governors of Medina]].{{sfn|Elad|2016|pp=337–338}}

==Assessment==
Ibn al-Zubayr adamantly opposed the caliphate becoming a dynastic inheritance of the Umayyads.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=77}} Instead, he advocated that the caliph should be chosen by ''{{Transliteration|ar|[[shura]]}}'' (consultation) among the Quraysh as a whole.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=77}} The Quraysh opposed the monopolization of power by the Banu Umayya and insisted power be distributed among all the Qurayshi clans.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=83}} However, other than this conviction, Ibn al-Zubayr did not sponsor any religious doctrine or political program, unlike the contemporary Alid and Kharijite movements.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}} By the time he made his claim to the caliphate, he had emerged as the leader of the disaffected Quraysh.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} According to historian [[H. A. R. Gibb]], Qurayshi resentment towards the Banu Umayya is evident as an underlying theme in the Islamic traditions about Ibn al-Zubayr's conflict with the Umayyads and Ibn al-Zubayr was the "principal representative" of the second generation of the Hejaz's elite Muslim families who chafed at the "gulf of power" between them and the ruling Umayyad house.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} Though Gibb describes Ibn al-Zubayr as "brave, but fundamentally self-seeking and self-indulgent", the hostility to the Umayyads in traditional Muslim sources led to a general description of him as a "model of piety".{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}} Nonetheless, a number of Muslim sources condemned him as jealous and harsh and particularly criticized the fatal abuse of his brother Amr and his imprisonment of Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}

Ibn al-Zubayr rallied opposition to the Umayyads in the Hejaz through his base in Mecca, Islam's holiest city, and his prestige as a first-generation Muslim with family ties to Muhammad.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}} He aimed to restore the Hejaz to its former political prominence;{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|p=200}} after the assassination of Uthman, the region's position as the political center of the Caliphate had been lost first to Kufa under Ali and then to Damascus under Mu'awiya.{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|pp=199–200}} To that end, Ibn al-Zubayr developed a strong association with the Ka'aba,{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}} which, combined with his control of Islam's second holiest city of Medina, furthered his prestige and gave his caliphate a holy character.{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|p=200}}{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=84}}

Ibn al-Zubayr rejected the offer of support from the Caliphate's core Syrian army partly because it would have obliged him to relocate to Damascus.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}} Other cities were available to him, but Ibn al-Zubayr opted to remain in Mecca,{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|p=200}} from which he issued directives to his supporters elsewhere in the Caliphate.{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=84}} This restricted him from exercising direct influence in the larger, more populated provinces, particularly Iraq, where his more worldly brother ruled with practical independence.{{sfn|Gibb|1960|p=55}}{{sfn|Kennedy|2004|p=84}} In Arabia, Ibn al-Zubayr's power had been largely confined to the Hejaz with the Kharijite leader Najda holding more influence in the greater part of the peninsula.{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|p=200}} Thus, Ibn al-Zubayr had virtually rendered himself a background figure in the movement that was launched in his name; in the words of historian [[Julius Wellhausen]], "the struggle turned round him nominally, but he took no part in it and it was decided without him".{{sfn|Wellhausen|1927|p=200}}

During his rule, Ibn al-Zubayr made significant alterations to the Ka'aba's structure, claiming that the changes were in line with the authority of Muhammad.{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}} He called himself the "fugitive at the sanctuary [Ka'aba]" while his Umayyad detractors referred to him as "the evil-doer at Mecca".{{sfn|Hawting|1986|p=49}}
