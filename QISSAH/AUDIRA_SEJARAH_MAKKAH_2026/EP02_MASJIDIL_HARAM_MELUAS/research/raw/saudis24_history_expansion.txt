 The Grand Mosque Through the Ages: A History of Expansion
 By Badr 
 Published on 2026/07/28 
 From the first expansion under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab to the massive King Abdullah project, a journey through the history of Masjid al-Haram's growth from a modest space around the Kaaba into the largest mosque in the world.
 Masjid al-Haram carries a history of successive expansions that mirrors the growth of the Muslim population across the centuries, evolving from a modest space around the Kaaba into the vast complex that receives millions of worshippers today.
 The first recorded expansion took place under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab around 17 AH (638 CE), when the rapidly growing number of Muslims made it necessary to widen the space around the Kaaba to manage the flow of Tawaf and prayer. Further expansions followed during the Umayyad and Abbasid periods. Caliph al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik is credited with raising the mosque's walls and introducing its first minarets, a feature that later became a defining element of major mosques worldwide. Under the Abbasids, Caliph Abu Ja'far al-Mansur enlarged the mosque in the mid-8th century, and Caliphs al-Mahdi and al-Hadi continued the expansion between 779 and 786 CE, bringing the mosque's area to roughly 28,000 square meters — more than triple its size within less than a century.
 During the Ottoman period, beginning in the early 16th century, Masjid al-Haram underwent extensive renovation and restoration work, with the Ottomans dedicating considerable resources to preserving and developing it, adding new domes and expanding its boundaries to accommodate growing numbers of pilgrims, particularly during Hajj. One of the most notable additions of this era came from Sultan Murad IV, who in 1629 added a stone arcade and three new minarets during a phase of rebuilding following earlier damage to parts of the mosque.
 With the establishment of the modern Saudi state, Masjid al-Haram entered a period of radical transformation. Between 1955 and 1976, the first major Saudi-era expansion added four new minarets and completely renewed the flooring, part of a broad modernization effort that kept pace with the rapid growth in pilgrim and Umrah visitor numbers through the mid-20th century.
 The second expansion, under King Fahd bin Abdulaziz, ran from 1982 to 1988 (with implementation details continuing into the early 1990s) and involved demolishing neighboring buildings to make room for a new wing and additional outdoor prayer areas. Once complete, the mosque's total area reached approximately 356,800 square meters, with capacity for around 820,000 worshippers at once.
 The largest expansion in the mosque's entire history, however, was the King Abdullah project, which began in 2008 and continued over many years, adding more than 300,000 square meters of new space. The project included multi-level extensions, new stairways and tunnels, two additional minarets, expanded areas for performing Tawaf, and comprehensive climate-control systems on an unprecedented scale. Thanks to this expansion, the mosque's capacity rose to over two million worshippers simultaneously — with some estimates citing capacity of up to 2.5 million during peak periods — while the total prayer area grew to roughly 912,000 square meters.
 Today, Masjid al-Haram is crowned by more than ten towering minarets spread across its various facades, the product of successive building phases across the Ottoman and Saudi eras, together forming one of the mosque's most recognizable visual features from a distance. The mosque today also includes multiple levels for Tawaf, elevators and escalators, and sophisticated crowd-management systems designed to accommodate growing numbers of worshippers year after year while maintaining their safety and comfort.
 The story of Masjid al-Haram's expansions — from a modest space around the Kaaba in the 7th century to a complex spanning nearly a million square meters today — embodies more than 1,400 years of continuous architectural response to the growth of Muslims worldwide, and it remains a story still being written, as the area surrounding the Haram continues to see ongoing development and improvement projects today.
