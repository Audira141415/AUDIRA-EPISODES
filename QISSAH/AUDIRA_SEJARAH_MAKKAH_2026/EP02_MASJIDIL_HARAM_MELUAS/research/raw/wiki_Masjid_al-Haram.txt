'''Masjid al-Haram''' ({{langx|ar|المسجد الحرام|translit=al-Masjid al-Ḥarām|lit=The Sacred Mosque}}),<ref>{{cite book |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=7jj4QLAN2coC&pg=PA69 |title=Sainthood: Its Manifestations in World Religions |page=69 |last=Denny |first=Frederick M. |editor-last1=Kieckhefer |editor-first1=Richard |editor-last2=Bond |editor-first2=George D. |publisher=University of California Press |date=9 August 1990 |isbn=9780520071896 |access-date=18 August 2019 |archive-date=18 September 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210918143013/https://books.google.com/books?id=7jj4QLAN2coC&pg=PA69 |url-status=live }}</ref> also known as the '''Great Mosque of Mecca''',<ref>{{Cite web |title=Great Mosque of Mecca {{!}} History, Expansion, & Facts |url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Great-Mosque-of-Mecca |access-date=2020-08-08 |website=Encyclopedia Britannica |language=en |archive-date=11 December 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191211000810/https://www.britannica.com/topic/Great-Mosque-of-Mecca |url-status=live }}</ref> is a [[mosque]] in [[Mecca]], Saudi Arabia, serving as the holiest and most important mosque in [[Islam]].<ref>{{cite book | url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tUmkEAAAQBAJ&dq=masjid+al+haram+holiest+masjid&pg=PT30 | title=Holy Lands of Abrahamic Religions: A Brief History and Pilgrimage | isbn=979-8-88869-631-6 | last1=Ravindran | first1=K. | date=5 January 2023 | publisher=Notion Press }}</ref><ref>{{cite book | url=https://books.google.com/books?id=EBlfEAAAQBAJ&dq=is+masjid+al+haram+the+most+important+masjid&pg=PA156 | title=Illustrated Dictionary of the Muslim World | date=15 January 2011 | publisher=Cavendish Square Publishing, LLC | isbn=978-0-7614-9966-4 }}</ref> {{As of|2026|post=,}} the Great Mosque is the [[List of largest mosques|largest mosque]] in the world.

The Great Mosque is considered, alongside the [[Quba Mosque]] in [[Medina]], among the earliest mosques in Islam.<ref>{{cite book |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=aMsvDAAAQBAJ |title=Historical Dictionary of Architecture |author=Palmer, A. It has undergone major renovations and expansions through the years.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/world-middle-east-34231620 |title=Mecca crane collapse: Saudi inquiry into Grand Mosque disaster |work=BBC News |date=12 September 2015 |access-date=12 February 2019 |archive-date=12 September 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150912214722/http://www.bbc.com/news/world-middle-east-34231620 |url-status=live }}</ref> It has passed through the control of various [[caliphs]], [[List of sultans of the Ottoman Empire|sultans]] and kings, and is now under the control of the [[King of Saudi Arabia]] who also took on the title [[Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques]].<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.fairobserver.com/region/middle_east_north_africa/mohammad-bin-salman-moderate-islam-saudi-arabia-reform-news-10621/ |title=Is Saudi Arabia Ready for Moderate Islam? – Latest Gulf News |website=fairobserver.com |date=3 November 2017 |language=en-US |access-date=2017-11-25 |archive-date=1 December 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171201044324/https://www.fairobserver.com/region/middle_east_north_africa/mohammad-bin-salman-moderate-islam-saudi-arabia-reform-news-10621/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

It encloses the vicinity of the [[Kaaba]] in Mecca, in the [[Hejaz|western region]] of the country. It is among the pilgrimage sites associated with the [[Hajj]], which every [[Muslim]] according to orthodox Islamic belief, must perform at least once in their lives if able. It is also the main site for the performance of ʿ[[Umrah]], the lesser pilgrimage that can be undertaken any time of the year. The rites of both pilgrimages include [[Tawaf|circumambulating the Kaaba]] within the mosque. The Great Mosque contains other important sites, such as the [[Black Stone]], the [[Zamzam Well]], [[Maqam Ibrahim]], and the hills of [[Safa and Marwa]].<ref>{{Cite quran|3|97 |t=y |s=ns}}</ref>

==History==
{{Further|Hejaz|History of Islam|History of Saudi Arabia|Islamic architecture}}

In the [[Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia|pre-Islamic religions of Arabia]] -spanning from the [[Levant]] to [[Yemen]], [[List of pre-Islamic Arabian deities|numerous deities]] —such as, [[Al-Lat]], [[Dhu al-Khalasa]], and [[al-Uzza]] (also occasionally referred to as [[Chaabou]]), [[Dushara]], [[Dhu Samawi]] etc.— were represented by [[Aniconism|aniconic]] cubic stone carvings or by cubic structures regarded as [[Kaabas|the abodes of these deities]]; furthermore, the existence of "sacred/haram zones" surrounding these sanctuaries and the widespread use of [[Ka'b|the name "Ka'b"]] to denote such sanctity, is well-established.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arabian-religion/Sanctuaries-cultic-objects-and-religious-practices-and-institutions|title=Arabian religion - Sanctuaries, Cultic Objects, Practices &#124; Britannica|website=www.britannica.com}}</ref> Although the cultural heritage of the pagan era was largely eradicated during Islam's struggle to establish [[Tawhid|Islamic monotheism]], historical research has uncovered traces of some [[Sanctuary|sanctuaries]] that were significant enough to rival the Kaaba in Mecca; nevertheless, the belief that there has always been only one Kaaba has become deeply ingrained in Muslim literary and religious tradition. Today, the Kaaba and the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca are identified as the direction of prayer; however, an archaeological document found in the [[Taif]] region suggests that this view is anachronistic from the perspective of traditional Islamic historiography, as a stone inscription discovered there dates the construction of the Masjid al-Haram to the year 78 AH.<ref>{{Cite web | url=https://www.islamic-awareness.org/history/islam/inscriptions/haram1.html | title=An Inscription Mentioning The Rebuilding Of Al-Masjid Al-Ḥarām, 78 AH / 697-698 CE | website=www.islamic-awareness.org}}</ref> Furthermore, a Quranic verse recounting [[Isra' and Mi'raj|Muhammad's night journey]] —an event often presented today as a miracle— to the [[Masjid al-Aqsa]]  offers clues suggesting that the ''Masjid al-Haram'' mentioned in the Quran might differ from the one located in Mecca. {{qref|17|1|c=y}}: 
{{blockquote|Glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from Masjid al-Haram to the [[Al-Aqsa Mosque|Masjid al-Aqsa]] whose surroundings We have blessed, so that We may show him some of Our signs. Indeed, He alone is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing}}

The significance of mosque structures from the early Islamic period that face in different directions continues to be a subject of serious debate between [[Revisionist school of Islamic studies|revisionist historians]] and conservatives.

===Era of Abraham and Ismael===
{{See also|Abraham in Islam|Hagar in Islam|Ishmael in Islam}}

According to Islamic tradition in the [[Quran]], [[Abraham in Islam|Abraham]], together with his son [[Ishmael in Islam|Ismael]], raised the foundations of a house,<ref>{{cite quran|2|127|t=y|s=ns}}</ref> which has been identified by commentators as the Kaaba. According to Islamic tradition, it is said that Allah showed Abraham the exact site, which was previously built by [[Adam in Islam|Adam]], very near to what is now the [[Well of Zamzam]]. After Abraham had built the Kaaba, an angel is said to have brought him the [[Black Stone]], a celestial stone that, according to tradition, had fallen from Heaven on the nearby hill [[Abu Qubays (mountain)|Abu Qubays]]. The Black Stone is believed by Islamic scholars to be the only remnant of the original structure made by Abraham.

After placing the Black Stone in the Eastern corner of the Kaaba, Abraham reportedly received a revelation in which God told the aged prophet that he should now go and proclaim the pilgrimage to mankind, so that men may come both from Arabia and from lands far away, on camel and on foot.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Quran 22:27 |url=http://legacy.quran.com/22/27-39 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161003073536/http://legacy.quran.com/22/27-39 |archive-date=3 October 2016 |access-date=1 October 2016}}</ref>

===Era of Muhammad===
{{Main|Muhammad in Islam}}

Upon Muhammad's [[Conquest of Mecca|victorious return to Mecca]] in 630 [[Common Era|CE]], he and a number of the [[Sahaba]] including Ali broke the idols in and around the Kaaba,<ref>{{cite quran|21|57 |e=58 |s=ns}}</ref> similar to what, according to the Quran, Abraham did in his homeland. === Rashidun era ===
The mosque has been expanded by [[Umar|caliph Umar]] in 637 CE by 1,500 m<sup>2</sup>, in response to the increasing number of Muslims, its area has reached a total of 3,600 m<sup>2</sup>, another expansion was carried out by [[Uthman|caliph Uthman]] where the covered porticoes have been introduced for the first time.<ref name=":7">{{Cite book |last=Casares |first=J. |url=https://www.google.com.sa/books/edition/Islamic_Heritage_Architecture_and_Art_II/t1H4DwAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&dq=the+expansion+of+almahdi+retained+the+mosque+size+until&pg=PA68&printsec=frontcover |title=Islamic Heritage Architecture and Art III |last2=Amarillo |first2=S. Dominguez |date=2020-08-11 |publisher=WIT Press |isbn=978-1-78466-393-3 |language=en}}</ref>

===Umayyad era===
In 682, the ruler of Mecca [[Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr]], who opposed the Umayyads following the death of [[Mu'awiya I]] carried an expansion that increased the total area to 7,465 m<sup>2</sup>,<ref name=":7" /> and following [[Siege of Mecca (692)|the reconquest of Mecca by the Umayyads]] in 692, the first major renovation to the mosque took place on the orders of [[Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan]].<ref>{{cite book |last=Guidetti |first=Mattia |title=In the Shadow of the Church: The Building of Mosques in Early Medieval Syria: The Building of Mosques in Early Medieval Syria |date=2016 |publisher=[[Brill Publishers|BRILL]] |isbn=9789004328839 |page=113 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=3n5yDQAAQBAJ&pg=PA113 |access-date=17 September 2017 |language=en |archive-date=20 November 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211120051251/https://books.google.com/books?id=3n5yDQAAQBAJ&pg=PA113 |url-status=live }}</ref> Before this renovation, which included the mosque's outer walls being raised, the mosque was a small open area with the Kaaba at the center. By the end of the 8th century, the mosque's old wooden columns had been replaced with marble columns and the wings of the prayer hall had been extended on both sides along with the addition of a [[minaret]] on the orders of [[Al-Walid I]].<ref>{{cite book |last1=Petersen |first1=Andrew |title=Dictionary of Islamic Architecture |date=2002 |publisher=Routledge |isbn=9781134613656 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=9A-EAgAAQBAJ&pg=PA180 |access-date=17 September 2017 |language=en |archive-date=20 November 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211120051315/https://books.google.com/books?id=9A-EAgAAQBAJ&pg=PA180 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last1=Ali |first1=Wijdan |author-link=Wijdan Ali |title=The Arab Contribution to Islamic Art: From the Seventh to the Fifteenth Centuries |date=1999 |publisher=American Univ in Cairo Press |isbn=9789774244766 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=rpUuqLPPKK4C |access-date=17 September 2017 |language=en |archive-date=20 November 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211120051252/https://books.google.com/books?id=rpUuqLPPKK4C |url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Abbasid era ===
The next expansion of the mosque was sponsored by the [[Abbasid Caliphate|Abbasid]] caliph [[al-Mansur]] between 754 and 757.<ref name=":16">{{Cite book |last=Petersen |first=Andrew |url=https://www.archnet.org/collections/126 |title=Dictionary of Islamic Architecture |publisher=Routledge |year=1996 |isbn=9781134613663 |pages=180 |language=en |chapter=Mecca (Makka)}}</ref> This was followed by a major expansion begun by his successor, Caliph [[al-Mahdi]], in 779. This work was completed shortly after him by his successor, Caliph [[al-Hadi]] ({{Reign|785|786}}),<ref name=":6">{{Cite book |last=Sardar |first=Ziauddin |url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/Mecca/XPKIBAAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&dq=mecca+mosque+abbasid&pg=PA366 |title=Mecca: The Sacred City |publisher=Bloomsbury Publishing USA |year=2014 |isbn=978-1-62040-268-9 |pages=366 |language=en}}</ref> which made the mosque's total area reach 28,000 m<sup>2</sup>. This was the largest expansion to the size of the mosque thus far. Two further extensions were completed by  caliph [[al-Mu'tadid]] in 894–5 and by  caliph [[al-Muqtadir]] in 918,<ref name=":6" /> but it retained its overall form from this period on until the later [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] renovations.<ref name=":16" />

===Mamluk era===
Significant structural repairs, expansions, and aesthetic enhancements were commissioned under successive [[Mamluk Sultanate|Mamluk sultans]], particularly in response to severe fires and flood damage. A devastating fire in 802 AH (1400 CE) severely damaged the mosque's colonnades and wooden roofs. 

Under Sultan [[Barquq]] and his son Sultan [[An-Nasir Faraj|al-Nasir Faraj]], extensive reconstruction work was initiated to rebuild the burned arcades, replace stone columns, and restore the ornate wooden ceilings around the Kaaba courtyard. Sultan al-Nasir Faraj also undertook major structural repairs to the Holy Kaaba itself in 813 AH (1410 CE) after further damage, reinforcing its masonry and renewing its external cladding and interior elements.<ref name="Website-1">{{Cite web |title=Archnet > Site > Masjid al-Haram |url=https://www.archnet.org/sites/3792 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251115205821/https://www.archnet.org/sites/3792 |archive-date=2025-11-15 |access-date=2026-10-18 |website=www.archnet.org |language=en-US}}</ref> The roof was patched with local wood from the [[Taif|Ta’if]] Mountains and the damaged marble columns were replaced with stone columns quarried from the nearby mountains of the Hijaz.<ref>Al-Hariri-Rifai, Mokhless. The Heritage of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, 200-212. Singapore: Eurasia Press, 1990.</ref><ref>Creswell, K.A.C. A Short Account of Early Muslim Architecture, 3-6. Aldershot: Scholar Press, 1989.</ref><ref>Damluji, Salma Samar ed. The Architecture of the Holy Mosque Makkah. London: Hazar Publishing Limited, 1998.</ref>

Sultan [[Qaitbay|Qaytbay]], who reigned from 872 to 901 AH (1468 to 1496 CE), was one of the most prolific patrons of the Hejaz. He financed comprehensive repairs to the Masjid al-Haram's arcades, domes, and minarets following seasonal flooding and structural wear, sending specialized craftsmen and builders directly from [[Cairo]] to oversee the work.<ref name="Website-1" />

During the late 9th to early 10th century AH (approx. late 15th and early 16th centuries), Mamluk rulers repaired and redesigned several minarets around the courtyard perimeter, incorporating distinctive [[Mamluk architecture|Cairene-Mamluk decorative styles]] characterized by multiple balconies, intricate stonework, and patterned finials.<ref name="Website-1" />

===Ottoman era===
In 1570, [[Ottoman Sultan|Sultan]] [[Selim II]] commissioned the chief architect [[Mimar Sinan]] to renovate the mosque. This renovation resulted in the replacement of the flat roof with domes decorated with [[Islamic calligraphy|calligraphy]] internally, and the placement of new support columns which are acknowledged as the earliest architectural features of the present mosque. These features are the oldest surviving parts of the building.

During heavy rains and flash floods in 1621 and 1629, the walls of the Kaaba and the mosque suffered extensive damage.<ref>{{cite book |author=James Wynbrandt |title=A Brief History of Saudi Arabia |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=eZkIXdsZpPsC&pg=PA101 |access-date=12 June 2013 |year=2010 |publisher=Infobase Publishing |isbn=978-0-8160-7876-9 |page=101 |archive-date=3 January 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140103080820/http://books.google.com/books?id=eZkIXdsZpPsC&pg=PA101 |url-status=live}}</ref> In 1629, during the reign of Sultan [[Murad IV]], the mosque was renovated. In the renovation of the mosque, a new stone arcade was added, three more minarets (bringing the total to seven) were built, and the marble flooring was retiled. This was the unaltered state of the mosque for nearly three centuries, the Ottoman era renovations did not expand the size of the mosque.

===Saudi era===

====First Saudi expansion====
The first major renovation under the Saudi kings was done between 1955 and 1976. In this renovation, four more minarets were added, the ceiling was refurnished, and the floor was replaced with artificial stone and marble. The Mas'a gallery (As-Safa and Al-Marwah) is included in the Mosque, via roofing and enclosures. During this renovation many of the historical features built by the Ottomans, particularly the support columns, were [[Destruction of early Islamic heritage sites in Saudi Arabia|demolished]].

On 20 November 1979, the Great Mosque was [[Grand Mosque seizure|seized by extremist insurgents]] who called for the overthrow of the Saudi dynasty. They took hostages and in the ensuing siege hundreds were killed. These events came as a shock to the Islamic world, as violence is strictly forbidden within the mosque.

====Second Saudi expansion====
The second Saudi renovations under [[King Fahd]], added a new wing and an outdoor prayer area to the mosque. The new wing, which is also for prayers, is reached through the King Fahd Gate. This extension was performed between 1986 and 1994.<ref>{{cite web |title=Gates of Masjid al-Haram |url=http://madainproject.com/gates_of_masjid_al_haram |website=Madain Project |access-date=17 March 2018 |archive-date=18 March 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180318054614/http://madainproject.com/gates_of_masjid_al_haram |url-status=live }}</ref>

1987 to 2005 saw the building of more minarets, the erecting of a [[King of Saudi Arabia|King's]] residence overlooking the mosque and more prayer area in and around the mosque itself. These developments took place simultaneously with those in [[Mount Arafat|Arafat]], [[Mina, Saudi Arabia|Mina]] and [[Muzdalifah]]. This extension also added 18 more gates, three domes corresponding in position to each gate and the installation of nearly 500 marble columns. Other modern developments added heated floors, air conditioning, escalators and a drainage system.{{Citation needed|date=February 2019}}

In addition, the King Fahd expansion includes 6 dedicated prayer halls for people with disabilities. These halls have ramps to facilitate entry and exit with wheelchairs, as well as dedicated paths and free electric and manual carts for their use.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://x.com/AlharamainSA/status/1702364005891797062|title=نحرص دائمًا على توفير مصليات مجهزة بأحدث التسهيلات والخدمات لضيوف بيت الله الحرام من #ذوي_الإعاقة. | language=ar | trans-title=We are always keen to provide prayer rooms equipped with the latest facilities and services for the guests of the Holy Mosque from #people_with_disabilities | website=x.com}}</ref>

==Pilgrimage==
{{main|Hajj|Umrah}}

The Great Mosque is the main setting for the Hajj and Umrah pilgrimages<ref>{{cite book |title=Hajj to Umrah: From A to Z |last=Mohammed |first=Mamdouh N. |year=1996 |publisher=Mamdouh Mohammed |isbn=0-915957-54-X |url=https://archive.org/details/hajjumrahfromtoz00moha}}</ref> that occur in the month of [[Dhu al-Hijja]] in the [[Islamic calendar]] and at any time of the year, respectively. The Hajj pilgrimage is one of the Pillars of Islam, required of all able-bodied Muslims who can afford the trip. In recent times, over 5&nbsp;million Muslims perform the Hajj every year.<ref>''General statistics of the Umrah season of 1436 A.H. until 24:00 hours, 28/09/1436 A.H. Total Number of the Mu`tamirs: 5,715,051'' {{cite web |title=General statistics of the Umrah season of 1436 A.H. |publisher=The Ministry of Hajj, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia |url=http://haj.gov.sa/en-us/Pages/default.aspx |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150813004502/http://www.haj.gov.sa/en-us/Pages/default.aspx |archive-date=13 August 2015 |url-status=dead}}</ref>

== Notable structures==
=== Kaaba ===
{{Main|Kaaba}}

[[File:The Kabah in the Grand Mosque of Makkah, Saudi Arabia (52501405646).jpg|thumb|The [[Kaaba]]]]
The [[Kaaba]] is a [[cuboid]]-shaped building in the centre of the Masjid al-Haram and is the [[List of significant religious sites#Islam|most sacred site]] in Islam.<ref name="eoi317">Wensinck, A. J; Ka`ba. [[Encyclopaedia of Islam]] IV p. 317</ref> It is the [[qibla]] ''(direction of prayer)'' for [[Muslims]] worldwide. It is the focal point of the [[Hajj]] and [[Umrah]] rites. The [[Pilgrimage|pilgrims]] perform the ''tawaf'' (circumambulation) around its exterior.<ref name="eoi317" /><ref>{{cite news |date=7 December 2008 |title=In pictures: Hajj pilgrimage |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/in_pictures/7769689.stm |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090120143537/http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/in_pictures/7769689.stm |archive-date=20 January 2009 |access-date=8 December 2008 |work=[[BBC News]]}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=As Hajj begins, more changes and challenges in store |url=http://www.altmuslim.com/a/a/a/as_hajj_begins_more_changes_and_challenges_in_store/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120111215050/http://www.altmuslim.com/a/a/a/as_hajj_begins_more_changes_and_challenges_in_store |archive-date=11 January 2012 |publisher=altmuslim}}</ref> The [[Kaaba]]'s fabric covering ([[kiswah]]) is replaced annually in a formal ceremony.
[[File:Kaaba-plan.svg|thumb|A technical drawing of the [[Kaaba]] showing dimensions and elements]]

==== Hatim ====
{{Main|Hijr Ismail}}

Adjacent to the [[Kaaba]] is the [[Hijr Ismail]], also known as the Hatim, a low semi-circular wall that marks an area historically regarded as part of the original footprint of the [[Kaaba]].<ref>{{Citation |last1=Ismail |first1=Hijr |title=Mecca Mukarramah |url=http://www.alummahworld.com/Mecca-Mukarramah |access-date=2015-11-04 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181229175253/https://alummahworld.com/Mecca-Mukarramah |archive-date=2018-12-29 |url-status=dead |language=English}}</ref>

==== Black Stone ====
{{Main|Black Stone}}

[[File:The Blackstone.jpg|thumb|The [[Black Stone]] is seen through a portal in the [[Kaaba]].]]
The [[Black Stone]] is set into the eastern corner of the Kaaba's external wall. Historically and culturally venerated in Islamic tradition, the stone today exists as several fragments within a silver frame and is a focal point of devotion during ''tawaf''.<ref>{{cite book |author=Shaykh Safi-Ar-Rahman Al-Mubarkpuri |title=Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum (The Sealed Nectar): Biography of the Prophet |publisher=Dar-As-Salam Publications |year=2002 |isbn=1-59144-071-8}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Mohamed |first=Mamdouh N. |url=https://archive.org/details/hajjumrahfromtoz00moha |title=Hajj to Umrah: From A to Z |publisher=Amana Publications |year=1996 |isbn=0-915957-54-X}}</ref>

=== Mataf (Tawaf area) ===
[[File:Masjid al-Haram 2022.jpg|thumb|The Mataf full of worshipers]]
The mataf is the open, circular, paved area immediately surrounding the [[Kaaba]] where ''tawaf'' (circumambulation) is performed. Over centuries the mataf has been enlarged and modernised, including multi-level and climate-controlled expansions to accommodate the large and growing numbers of pilgrims that visit during [[Hajj]] and throughout the year.<ref name="Long16">{{cite book |last=Long |first=David E. |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=2Uk3Gh6xrUUC&pg=PA16 |title=The Hajj Today: A Survey of the Contemporary Pilgrimage to Makkah |publisher=[[SUNY Press]] |year=1979 |isbn=0-87395-382-7 |page=16 |access-date=29 August 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200219105043/https://books.google.com/books?id=2Uk3Gh6xrUUC&pg=PA16 |archive-date=19 February 2020 |url-status=live}}</ref> Major 20th- and 21st-century Saudi expansion programmes substantially increased the mataf’s capacity.<ref name=":3" /><ref name=":5" />

=== Maqam Ibrahim ===
{{Main|Maqam Ibrahim}}

[[File:Maqam Ibrahim, Makkah.jpg|thumb|The enclosure of [[Maqam Ibrahim]] in ''Mataf'' area]]
[[Maqam Ibrahim]] is a small square stone associated in Islamic tradition with the [[Abraham]] ([[Abraham in Islam|Ibrahim]]). It is displayed near the [[Kaaba]] under a protective enclosure.<ref>M.J. Kister, "Maḳām Ibrāhīm," p.105, ''The Encyclopaedia of Islam'' (new ed.), vol. VI (Mahk-Mid), eds. Bosworth et al., Brill: 1991, pp. 104–107.</ref> According to Islamic tradition, the [[Petrosomatoglyph|imprint on the stone]] came from Abraham's feet.<ref>{{cite book |last=Peters |first=F.E. |title=The Hajj |date=1994 |publisher=Princeton University Press |isbn=9780691026190 |place=Princeton, New Jersey |pages=16–17 |chapter=Another Stone: The Maqam Ibrahim |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=EK5MqskDYC0C&pg=PA16}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |date=25 September 2016 |title=Maqam-e-Ibrahim shines ... like visitors' faith |url=http://www.arabnews.com/node/989361/saudi-arabia}}</ref> It is the only standing historic structure in the ''Mataf'' area out of at least six other, which were removed to clear the area for the circumambulation (''tawaf'').<ref name="madainmataf">{{cite web |title=Mataf |url=https://madainproject.com/mataf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240617084209/https://madainproject.com/mataf |archive-date=17 June 2024 |access-date=9 July 2024 |website=Madain Project}}</ref>

==Destruction of heritage sites==
{{Main|Destruction of early Islamic heritage sites in Saudi Arabia}}

Many of the Mosque’s historic and traditional structures were removed over time by Saudi-[[Wahhabism|Wahhabi]] authorities ever since they took control of the site in December 1924. There has been controversy that the expansion projects of the mosque and Mecca itself are causing harm to early Islamic heritage. Many ancient buildings, some more than a thousand years old, have been demolished to make room for the expansions, starting with the First Modern Saudi Expansion (1950-1955).<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/mecca-for-the-rich-islams-holiest-site-turning-into-vegas-2360114.html |work=The Independent |first=Jerome |last=Taylor |date=24 September 2011 |title=Mecca for the Rich: Islam's holiest site turning into Vegas |access-date=5 December 2017 |archive-date=16 June 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170616174556/http://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/mecca-for-the-rich-islams-holiest-site-turning-into-vegas-2360114.html |url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.islamicpluralism.org/467/dr-sami-angawi-on-wahhabi-desecration-of-makkah |title=Dr.Sami Angawi on Wahhabi Desecration of Makkah |access-date=28 November 2010 |last=Abou-Ragheb |first=Laith |date=12 July 2005 |publisher=Center for Islamic Pluralism |archive-date=22 July 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160722073326/http://www.islamicpluralism.org/467/dr-sami-angawi-on-wahhabi-desecration-of-makkah |url-status=live }}</ref>

Historically, the open courtyard (''Mataf'') surrounding the Kaaba contained several Ottoman- and Mamluk- era architectural structures that were removed during 20th century expansions of Masjid al-Haram:

# '''The Four ''Maqams''''': Four domed prayer pavilions surrounding the Kaaba represented the four major Sunni schools of jurisprudence (''[[Madhhab|mazhabs]]''): [[Hanafi school|Hanafi]], [[Shafi'i school|Shafi'i]], [[Maliki school|Maliki]], and [[Hanbali school|Hanbali]]. Historically, each school maintained its own designated imam who led congregation prayers from its respective pavilion. In the 1950s, these pavilions were dismantled under Saudi administration to unify congregational prayers behind a single imam of the Hanbali madhhab, and to allegedly clear space for pilgrims performing the circumambulation (''Tawaf'').
# '''The Zamzam Well Pavilion (''Qubbat Zamzam'')''': The Well of Zamzam was traditionally enclosed within a two-story domed building where pilgrims gathered to access and drink the sacred water. The pavilion was demolished in 1963 to reduce congestion near the Kaaba. The wellhead was initially converted into an underground chamber before being completely sealed and paved over in 2003, with the water redirected to distribution taps and cooling stations throughout the complex.
# '''The Dome of the Library (''Qubbat al-Kutub'')''': Located opposite the Zamzam pavilion, this twin-dome structure (also known as ''Qubbat al-Farashin'') was historically used to store Koran manuscripts, mosque lighting apparatuses, and carpets. It was dismantled alongside surrounding courtyard structures during the mid-20th-century Saudi expansion.
# '''Ottoman Marble Pulpit (''Minbar'')''': An intricately carved white marble pulpit, installed during the [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman period]] and used by the imam for [[Friday prayer|Friday sermons]], stood adjacent to the ''Mataf''. It was removed during modern expansions to streamline movement around the sanctuary.

== Sound System==
In 1998, Masjid al-Haram in Mecca received a major [[Audio equipment|sound-system]] upgrade of exceptional scale and complexity. The project delivered a fully computerized system capable of transmitting the imam’s voice clearly and without distortion to millions of worshippers, both inside the mosque and up to three and a half miles away, while incorporating extreme redundancy so that prayer services can continue uninterrupted even if multiple microphones fail, a control room catches fire, or two power plants are lost. 

Because non-Muslims are forbidden to enter Mecca, the work was carried out remotely. American automation specialist companies designed and programmed the system and trained a team of Saudi technicians to install and operate it. Approximately 6,000 custom-designed speakers and fiberglass horns were supplied along with the amplifiers. Twenty-five million feet of wiring were carefully laid in existing joints and crevices to avoid damaging the mosque’s centuries-old marble walls.

The new system, which replaced a twenty-year-old manual installation, is controlled by fourteen main computers (seven in a backup room), 380 amplifiers, and 126 microphones arranged at seven locations around the Kaaba. Each microphone stand carries nine microphones to cover standing, kneeling, and prostrate positions, with additional spare stands available. Multiple layers of backup power and equipment ensure continuous operation five times a day, every day of the year.

The project was a gift to the mosque from a prominent Saudi construction company. After two years of design meetings held in Cairo and London, the system started being installed in late 1998, with a demonstration scheduled for King Fahd.<ref>https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1998-dec-02-fi-49707-story.html</ref>
