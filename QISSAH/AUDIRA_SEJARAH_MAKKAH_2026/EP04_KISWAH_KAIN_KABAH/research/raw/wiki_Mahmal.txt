A '''mahmal''' ({{langx|ar|مَحْمَل|maḥmal}}) is a ceremonial passenger-less [[Litter (vehicle)|litter]] that was carried on a camel among caravans of pilgrims on the [[Hajj]], the pilgrimage to [[Mecca]] which is a sacred duty in [[Islam]]. It symbolised the political power of the [[sultan]]s who sent it, demonstrating their custody of Islam's holy sites. Each mahmal had an intricately embroidered textile cover, or ''sitr''.<ref name="irwin">{{cite book |author-link1=Robert Irwin (writer)|last1=Irwin |first1=Robert |editor1-last=Porter |editor1-first=Venetia |title=Hajj : journey to the heart of Islam |date=2012 |publisher=British Museum Press |location=London |isbn=9780714111766 |pages=140–145 |chapter=Journey to Mecca: A History (Part 2)}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=Mahmal |url=https://www.khalilicollections.org/collections/hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage/khalili-collection-hajj-and-the-arts-of-pilgrimage-mahmal-txt442/ |website=Khalili Collections |access-date=13 December 2022}}</ref> The tradition dates back at least to the 13th century and ended in the mid-20th. There are many descriptions and photographs of mahmals from 19th century observers of the Hajj.<ref name=":2">{{Cite book|last1=Davidson |first1=Linda Kay|chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=YVYkrNhPMQkC&pg=PA364|title=Pilgrimage : from the Ganges to Graceland : an encyclopedia|last2=Gitlitz|first2=David Martin|date=2002|publisher=ABC-CLIO|isbn=1-57607-543-5|location=Santa Barbara, Calif.|pages=364|chapter=Mahmal|oclc=52500776}}</ref>

== History ==
The word "mahmal" comes from the root حمل (''ḥ-m-l'', "''to carry''").<ref name="MahmalRevisited" /> A mahmal consists of a wooden frame made to fit on a camel, with a pointed top.<ref name="MahmalRevisited" /> There were textile coverings placed over it: an ornate processional covering and others for everyday use.<ref name="irwin" /> These coverings are known as the ''kiswah'' or ''sitr al-mahmal''.<ref name="MahmalRevisited" /> The earliest surviving covers, from the [[Mamluk Sultanate]], are yellow, but later instances are red or green.<ref name="irwin" /> The embroidered decoration would include the [[tughra]] (seal) of the Sultan as well as verses from the [[Quran]].<ref name="irwin" />
[[File:Khalili Collection Hajj and Arts of Pilgrimage arc-pt-0123.jpg|thumb|right|The return of the mahmal from Mecca to Cairo. Wood engraving, 1893]]
The first recorded sending of a mahmal was by [[Baibars]], who was Sultan of Egypt from 1260 to 1277.<ref name="irwin" /> Mahmals were sent from [[Cairo]], [[Damascus]], [[Yemen]], [[Hyderabad]], [[Darfur]], and the [[Timurid Empire]] in different periods.<ref name="MahmalRevisited" /> Although the main pilgrim caravan from Egypt departed from Cairo, a separate caravan with its own mahmal departed annually from [[Asyut]] from the late 14th century.<ref>{{Cite book|last=Le Quesne|first=Charles|title=The Hajj: collected essays|publisher=British Museum|year=2013|isbn=978-0-86159-193-0|editor-last=Porter|editor-first=Venetia|location=London|page=79|chapter=Hajj Ports of the Red Sea: A Historical and Archaeological Overview|oclc=857109543|editor-last2=Saif|editor-first2=Liana}}</ref>

On 18 June 1926, Egyptian soldiers playing music while escorting the mahmal, were confronted by angry [[Najd|Najdi Muslims]], who disliked the ''mahmal'' as an innovation (''[[Bid'ah]]'') and considered [[Islam and music|music un-Islamic]]. The Egyptians fired on them, killing 25.<ref>{{Cite book|last=Tagliacozzo and Toorawa|first=Eric and Shawkat M.|title=The Hajj: Pilgrimage in Islam|publisher=Cambridge University Press|year=2015|isbn=978-1107612808|location=Cambridge|pages=207}}</ref> The tradition of sending annual mahmals to Mecca ended in 1926, although they were paraded in Cairo until 1952.<ref name="MahmalRevisited" />
[[File:Khalili Collection Hajj and Arts of Pilgrimage ARC pp-0163.jpg|right|upright|thumb|Mahmal, Egypt, circa 1886. Photograph by the [[Abdullah Frères]]]]
The arrival of the mahmal in Mecca was a significant occasion which local people and pilgrims came out to watch.<ref name="irwin" /> Before entry to the city, the simple textiles which had covered the mahmal on its journey across the desert or sea were replaced with the ornate, colourful ''kiswah''.<ref name=":2" /> Mahmals from different countries would vie for the best position in front of the [[Kaaba]].<ref name=":2" /> A mahmal returning from Mecca to its city of origin was regarded as carrying ''[[barakah]]'' (blessing) which could be transferred by touch. As the procession returned to a city, parents brought out their children to touch the mahmal, and people briefly put their handkerchiefs inside it.<ref name=":0">{{Cite book|last=McGregor|first=Richard J. A.|url=|title=Islam and the devotional object : seeing religion in Egypt and Syria|publisher=|year=2020|isbn=978-1-108-59423-3|location=Cambridge, United Kingdom|pages=52–53|oclc=1128065777}}</ref>

== Origins ==
The tradition's origin is not known for certain. The Turkish traveler [[Evliya Çelebi]] wrote that the earliest mahmals carried relics of the prophet [[Muhammad]] including his shoes, gown and bowl, and hence that the mahmal was seen as representing the prophet's grave.<ref name=":0" /> Another theory is that [[Shajar al-Durr]], the first [[Sultana (title)|Sultana]], made the Hajj journey in a colourful litter and that subsequent leaders kept up the tradition, with the litter empty, after her death in the 13th century.<ref name="MahmalRevisited" /><ref name=":1" />

== Contents of the mahmal ==
Some sources say the mahmal was completely empty, while others describe it containing a [[Quran]] or other holy book. An anonymous account from 1575 describes a mahmal carrying "the Quran all written in great letters of gold".<ref name="MahmalRevisited" />
In the ''[[Anis Al-Hujjaj]]'' (''Pilgrim's companion''), a detailed record of a Hajj undertaken in 1677, the Damascus mahmal is depicted containing a Quran on a stand.<ref name="MahmalRevisited">{{Cite book|last=Porter|first=Venetia|title=The Hajj: collected essays|publisher=British Museum|year=2013|isbn=978-0-86159-193-0|editor-last=Porter|editor-first=Venetia|location=London|page=199|chapter=The Mahmal Revisited|oclc=857109543|editor-last2=Saif|editor-first2=Liana}}</ref> Swiss traveler [[John Lewis Burckhardt]] observed the Egyptian caravan in 1814 and wrote that a book of prayers, but not the Quran, would be carried in the mahmal. On its return from Mecca, people would kiss and rub their foreheads against the book to receive blessings.<ref name="MahmalRevisited" /> In the 1830s, British traveler [[Edward William Lane]] described a mahmal that was empty but had two copies of the Quran — one as a scroll and the other bound as a book — attached externally to the top of the mahmal in silver-gilt cases.<ref name=":1">{{Cite book|last=Lane|first=Edward William|author-link=Edward William Lane|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=eVkPAQAAMAAJ|title=An Account of the Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians. Written in Egypt during the years 1833–1835|date=1890|publisher=Ward, Lock and Company|isbn=|location=|pages=405|language=en|oclc=1153735791}}</ref>
