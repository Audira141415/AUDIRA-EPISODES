Zamzam, the World's Most Sacred Groundwater Well: Mecca, Saudi Arabia
Copyright and License information
Revised 2026 Aug 12; Received 2026 Aug 12; Accepted 2026 Aug 14; Issue date 2026 Sep-Oct.
All rights reserved, including rights for text and data mining and training of artificial intelligence technologies or similar technologies.
The Zamzam well is first recorded in the Torah and Bible (2012), Genesis chapter 21 verse 19. Although not specifically mentioned in the Q'uran it is named in several Hadiths (sayings of the prophet Muhammad) including the referenced Hadith 3062 (2007) thereby becoming a holy site for muslims. Abraham had cast out his bondwoman Hagar into the Arabian desert with their son Ismail circa 1800 BCE (nearly 4000 years ago). Their limited supply of water was soon spent and a desperate search for water was made before a well was revealed. This is recognized in Islamic tradition as the Zamzam well, which is located 21 m from the Kaaba within the Al‐Haram mosque (masjid) in Mecca. The Kaaba, the black cuboid structure at the center of the mosque, was rebuilt by Abraham and is the most sacred of Islamic sites faced by all muslims during their daily prayers (Figure 1).
Mecca. Masjid al‐Haram and the Kaaba at center. Saudi Press Agency. June 2024.
Again citing Islamic tradition, the original Kaaba was constructed by Adam circa 4000 BCE, which was a time of much higher rainfall than at present. The source of water for this original Kaaba may have been a stream along what is now Wadi Ibrahim but could also have been a shallow adjacent well giving a potential age of more than 6000 years for the Zamzam well.
During her desperate search for water Hagar ran backwards and forwards between the hills of Safa and Marwah in Mecca, both having approximate elevations of 360 m above sea level (masl) at their summits. This search, termed Saa'i, is re‐enacted annually by some 20 million pilgrims in the Hajj and Umrah rituals, crossing the 394 m distance between the feet of two hills seven times in a covered passageway bordering the mosque. Each pilgrim returns home with 5 L of holy Zamzam water which has perceived health benefits. The annual number of Hajj pilgrims is restricted to about 1.5 million during a specified schedule based on lunar predictions while no limits are placed on the Umrah which is open throughout the year and attracts up to 18.5 million pilgrims.
The depth to water in the well ranges between 0.74 and 2.15 m below the floor of the chamber in which it is now housed. Current average annual rainfall in Mecca is about 100 mm, which generally manifests as short‐term thunderstorms. From the time of the Kaaba's first construction in 4000 BCE to the time of the Zamzam revelation in about 1800 BCE, there have been marked changes in rainfall patterns. During the Holocene humid period, the area was marked by monsoons, rivers, freshwater lakes, and a diverse ecosystem. The shift to aridity began about 4000 BCE, and rainfall has since decreased rapidly, punctuated by a wetter period coinciding with the Little Ice Age between about 1400 and 1850 BCE. During those wetter periods, water table levels at the site of the Zamzam well would have been much closer to the surface.
The Zamzam well lies within the Al‐Haram mosque along Wadi Ibrahim (Figure 2). The dry water courses in Saudi Arabia are referred to as wadis and characterized by shallow water tables, unlike the extensive fossil aquifers which lie further inland. The alluvial sediments deposited in the wadis comprise permeable sands and gravels, which provide a readily accessible groundwater supply. Mecca lies within the Arabian‐Nubian Shield with a predominance of Neoproterozoic metamorphosed volcanic rocks (quartz diorite and tonalite) which are exposed within the Wadi Ibrahim catchment. These are fractured and have a high secondary porosity. Both the alluvium and the bedrock are intersected in the Zamzam well and contribute to a sustainable water supply, which has been sourced by pilgrims for millennia.
Wadi Ibrahim catchment (Hidayatulloh and Bahrawi 2025).
Floods have been a regular occurrence along Wadi Ibrahim and at times have inundated the Al‐Haram mosque and the Kaaba (Figure 3). After the severe floods of 1941 when a pilgrim actually swam around the Kaaba seven times, additional protection has been afforded first by raising the floor of the mosque and more recently by installation of underground tunnels and stormwater networks.
The flood of 1941 (Hidayatulloh et al. 2026). Zamzam well superstructure to the right of Kaaba.
The Zamzam well (21°25′21.5″N, 39°49′35.5″E) sits at an elevation of 310 masl and is 30 m (about 100 ft) deep. The upper part down to 12.80 m is lined with stone through the wadi alluvium with a diameter of 2.66 m; the lower 17.20 m has over the centuries been progressively excavated in rock with a diameter of 1.08 m and unlined. In 1964 the well was relocated 2.7 m from the floor of the mosque to an underground chamber and is now surrounded by glass plates permitting a clear view (Figure 4). The water table level ranges between 0.74 and 2.15 m deep below the floor of the chamber and the well can yield 18.5 L/s.
The Zamzam well (Mohammad Bahareth 2013).
The groundwater is slightly alkaline with a pH of 7.65 and its chemistry reflects the provenance of volcanic rocks within the Wadi Ibrahim catchment. The major anions and cations are listed in Table 1 (Donia and Mortada 2021). The water is believed to have significant health benefits, muslims always pray before drinking the water.
The 20 million annual pilgrims to Mecca each return home with 5 L of holy Zamzam water equivalent to 100,000 kL. Daily capacity of the total system is 1000 m3/day which is enough to meet this and other demands and is within the recharge capacity of the Wadi Ibrahim aquifers.
Mecca has a current permanent resident population of 2.386 million, which experiences monthly peaks of up to two million during the pilgrimages. It has the most sophisticated sewerage system in Saudi Arabia comprising gravity and large trunk sewers with pumping stations. Approximately 650,000 m3 is treated each day and is reused for irrigation, landscaping and industrial purposes but not for drinking. The Zamzam aquifer is carefully protected with sewers in the central area near the Al‐Haram mosque sealed to prevent leakage. The municipal water supply is 97% remineralized desalinated water sourced from desalination plants at the Red Sea near Jeddah 95 km to the southwest together with 3% groundwater from wadi aquifers near Mecca. The groundwater in these aquifers has a total dissolved solids (TDS) up to 1750 mg/L. The combined TDS averages 150 mg/L compared to 830 mg/L for Zamzam groundwater and confirms there are no leakages to the aquifer which could impact water table levels or quality.
The population of Mecca has increased significantly in recent times from 1.294 million in 2000 to 2.017 million in 2020 due to the city's status as a holy site. This has been accompanied by intensive development of high‐rise buildings and road networks intensifying runoff generation and placing increased stress on recharge to the Wadi Ibrahim aquifer. The Zamzam Studies and Research Centre was established in 2000 by the Saudi Geological Survey (https://sgs.gov.sa/en/) to address these challenges. The Centre monitors all projects surrounding Wadi Ibrahim and prepares engineering solutions to preserve and protect the Zamzam water. It imposes building controls within the Wadi Ibrahim catchment to ensure that structures meet engineering standards for prevention of impacts on recharge and groundwater quality. The Zamzam groundwater quality is monitored several times daily to ensure that any adverse changes from external sources such as leaks from municipal water or sewage networks, should they occur, can be detected early and corrected. The royal commission for the Zamzam water project was established by King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz in 2011.
This essay provides a comprehensive description of the Zamzam well in Mecca and provides a historical perspective dating back 6000 years. Its revelation to Abraham's wife and their son Ismail is commemorated as a ritual in the annual pilgrimages by 20 million devotees. It can rightly be recognized as the world's most sacred groundwater site.
