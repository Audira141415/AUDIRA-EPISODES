The '''Canal of Zubaidah''' ({{langx|ar|عين زبيدة|translit='Ain Zubaida}}), also anglicized as the '''Canal of Zubaydah''', is a system of covered [[Aqueduct (bridge)|aqueduct]]s (''[[qanat]]'') outside of [[Mecca]], Saudi Arabia, that historically provided water to [[hajj]] pilgrims. Built under the direction of [[Abbasid]] queen [[Zubaidah bint Ja'far]], the canal was completed in the early 9th century and allowed water to be transported from two [[wadi]]s{{mdash}}Wadi Hunayn and, later, Wadi Numan{{mdash}}to [[Mount Arafat]], [[Muzdalifah]], and [[Mina, Saudi Arabia|Mina]]. Although requiring regular repairs, the system continued to function for over a thousand years, carrying up to {{convert|40000|m3}} of water per day. In 1974, amid high levels of water pumping and urban expansion, the system was rendered defunct. In the 21st century, efforts have been undertaken to preserve and revitalize the canal.

The Canal of Zubaidah is located east of [[Mecca]], a city in Saudi Arabia.{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=218}} Historically, it consisted of two covered aqueducts, or ''[[qanat]]'', that drew water from the Wadi Hunayn and, later, the Wadi Numan;{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} the aqueducts are known respectively as the Canal of Hunayn and the Canal of Arafat.{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}} These works were carved into the local [[alluvium]] and weathered [[bedrock]], with open portions constructed of stone where necessitated by the topography.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} [[Lime mortar]] was used to reinforce the structure.{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}} Stone-lined access holes for maintenance were installed at {{convert|50|m|adj=on}} intervals;{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|pp=218, 220}} access to water was provided through 130 wells, which varied in size and shape.{{sfn|Hussain|Abu-Rizaiza|Habib|Ashfaq|2008|p=342}} A small slope{{mdash}}approximately 1 in 3000{{mdash}}allowed water to flow under its own gravity.{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=220}}

Cisterns for the earliest canal were concentrated near Mecca.{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=219}} The later canal followed a meandering path between hills.{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}} It originated at Wadi Numan, where four or five wells up to {{convert|35|m}} deep were dug.{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=219}} From there it went to [[Mount Arafat]], where storage tanks and access holes for drinking were installed;{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}} the majority of access was provided at this point.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} The canal then followed an underground path northward around Wadi Urahna to [[Muzdalifah]], emerging near the [[Al-Mash'ar al-Haram Mosque]]. Water could be accessed there and transported further to [[Mina, Saudi Arabia|Mina]].<ref>{{harvnb|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=220}}; {{harvnb|Khurshid|2012}}</ref> Combined, both systems measured some {{convert|35|km}} in length.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}}

==History==
===Construction===
The Canal of Zubaidah was built under the command of Queen [[Zubaidah bint Ja'far]] (died 831 CE), the wife of Caliph [[Harun al-Rashid]] of the [[Abbasid Caliphate]].{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=218}} During her [[hajj]] pilgrimage, Zubaidah realized the need for potable water during the journey. She thus funded the construction of a covered canal to provide water to pilgrims travelling between Mount Arafat, Mina, and Muzdalifah.{{sfn|Siraj|Tayab|2017|p=34}} The construction of the aqueduct was one of several projects funded by Zubaidah to facilitate the hajj pilgrimage, another being the refurbishing of the [[Zubaydah Trail]].{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} This trail, which connects [[Baghdad]] in modern-day Iraq with Mecca, is often mistakenly conflated with the canal.{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}}

Construction began at the end of the 8th century,{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=218}} and concluded in the early 9th century; sources vary as to the year of completion, with some citing 801 CE and others citing 809{{ndash}}810 CE.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} Two sites were chosen, both of which benefitted from a shallow [[water table]].{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=218}} Initially, the canal drew water from Wadi Hunayn; for this, storage tanks were built in Mecca,{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=218}} while existing [[date palm]] farms were cleared to allow construction. A pool was constructed at Mount Arafat to allow the ready collection of water.{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}} 

As water levels dropped, and as flooding filled the Canal of Hunayn with silt and sand,{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}} a connection to Wadi Numan was built. This leg followed a {{convert|27|km|adj=on}} route connecting with Arafat and the al-Azizyah quarter of Mecca.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} Numerous water stations were installed to support pilgrims,{{sfn|Siraj|Tayab|2017|p=34}} including surface channels and reservoirs.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} The entire project was reported to have cost 1.7 million ''[[mithqal]]'' ({{convert|7225|kg|disp=sqbr}}) of gold.{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}}

===Subsequent history===
[[File:Pilgrims collecting water from the Canal of Zubaidah, 1928.jpg|thumb|Pilgrims collecting water, 1928]]
The canal required frequent upkeep, with the first repairs conducted under Caliph [[al-Mutawakkil]] ([[Reign|r.]] 847{{ndash}}861) and later maintenance conducted every thirty years.{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}} Many early repairs were completed by the rulers of nearby Muslim nations,{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}} including Emir [[Hasan ibn Ajlan]] of Mecca and Sultans [[Qaitbay]] and [[Al-Ashraf Qansuh al-Ghuri]] of the [[Mamluk Sultanate]].{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}} 

Regular maintenance was also conducted by the [[Ottoman Empire]], which conducted multiple surveys of the system.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} Under the Ottomans, [[Slavery in Saudi Arabia|slave labour]] was used for much of maintenance. In the mid-16th century, major repairs were ordered by [[Mihrimah Sultan (daughter of Suleiman I)|Mihrimah Sultan]] and involved a workforce of almost a thousand people.{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=279}} Sultan [[Mustafa III]] initiated a three-year restoration in 1757, overseen by Feyzullah Efendi, with a budget of 86,000 [[kuruş]]. In 1819, Sultan {{nowrap|[[Mahmud II]]}} ordered another restoration, granting significant powers to [[Muhammad Ali of Egypt]].{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=280}} 

By the 1870s, the system was again in a state of disrepair. A number of [[Indian Muslims]] thus coordinated fundraising efforts throughout the [[Islamic world]], with work continuing from 1876 through 1883.{{sfn|Bilge|1991|p=280}} Another major restoration project was undertaken by [[Ibn Saud]] in 1928 after flooding damaged the canal, blocking it with silt and other materials.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} Although it was restored to functionality, with its capacity recorded at {{convert|40000|m3}} per day,{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=220}} by the middle of the century continued pumping and urban development had disrupted canal's ability to recharge.{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} 

After providing water to hajj pilgrims for more than a thousand years,{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=218}} the canal was rendered defunct in 1974,{{sfn|Madain Project, Canal of Zubaida}} though advances in [[aquifer]] extraction had allowed Mecca's water needs to be met.{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}} Elements of the canal remain visible to pilgrims {{as of|2017|lc=yes}},{{sfn|Siraj|Tayab|2017|p=34}} with the remaining infrastructure measured at approximately {{convert|18|km}}.{{sfn|Al-Bassam|Zaidi|2016|p=220}} The surviving elements have been protected by the Saudi government,{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}} and in 2004 research was conducted into the viability of revitalizing the network.{{sfn|Hussain|Abu-Rizaiza|Habib|Ashfaq|2008|p=343}} In the 2010s, plans were developed to restore parts of the canal and build a museum to introduce visitors to the canal and its history.{{sfn|Khurshid|2012}}
