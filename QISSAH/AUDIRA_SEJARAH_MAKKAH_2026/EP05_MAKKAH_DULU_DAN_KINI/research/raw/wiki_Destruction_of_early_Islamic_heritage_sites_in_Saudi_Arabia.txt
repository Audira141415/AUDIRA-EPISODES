The destruction of [[Historic site|heritage sites]] associated with [[Early history of Islam|early Islam]] is an ongoing phenomenon that has occurred mainly in the [[Hejaz]] region of western [[Saudi Arabia]], particularly around the [[Holiest sites in Islam|two holiest cities of Islam]], [[Mecca]] and [[Medina]].<ref name="Britannica">{{cite encyclopedia |encyclopedia=[[Encyclopædia Britannica]] |title=Wahhābī (Islamic movement) |url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Wahhabi |date=9 June 2020 |access-date=8 September 2020 |publisher=[[Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.]] |location=[[Edinburgh]] |quote=Because [[Wahhabism|Wahhābism]] prohibits the veneration of shrines, tombs, and sacred objects, many sites associated with the [[early history of Islam]], such as the homes and graves of [[Companions of the Prophet|companions]] of [[Muhammad]], were demolished under Saudi rule. [[Preservationist]]s have estimated that as many as 95 percent of the historic sites around [[Mecca]] and [[Medina]] have been razed. |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200626201633/https://www.britannica.com/topic/Wahhabi |archive-date=26 June 2020 |url-status=live}}</ref> The demolition has focused on mosques, burial sites, homes, and historical locations associated with the [[Islamic prophet]] [[Muhammad]], his [[Companions of the Prophet|companions]], and many of the founding personalities of [[Historiography of early Islam|early Islamic history]] under the [[Saudi government]].<ref name="Britannica" /><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/medina-saudis-take-a-bulldozer-to-islams-history-8228795.html|title=Medina: Saudis take a bulldozer to Islam's history|work=The Independent|date=26 October 2012|access-date=14 November 2014|archive-date=6 September 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170906161013/http://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/medina-saudis-take-a-bulldozer-to-islams-history-8228795.html|url-status=live}}</ref> In Saudi Arabia, many of the demolitions have officially been part of the continued expansion of the [[Masjid al-Haram]] in Mecca and the [[Al-Masjid al-Nabawi|Prophet's Mosque]] in Medina and their auxiliary service facilities in order to accommodate the ever-increasing number of Muslims performing the pilgrimage (''[[hajj]]'').<ref>{{cite web|url=https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/Cultural-genocide-of-Islamic-heritage-in-Saudi-Arabia-riles-Sunni-Sufis/articleshow/17564283.cms|archive-url=https://archive.today/20130411040819/http://articles.timesofindia.indiatimes.com/2012-12-11/india/35749248_1_islamic-heritage-islamic-cooperation-aiumb-general-secretary|url-status=live|title='Cultural genocide of Islamic heritage' in Saudi Arabia riles Sunni Sufis |date=11 April 2013|work=[[The Times of India]]|archive-date=11 April 2013}}</ref>

== History ==
Much of the [[Arabian Peninsula]] was politically unified in 1932 under the third and current Saudi state, the [[Kingdom of Saudi Arabia]]. The military campaign led by [[Ibn Saud|King Abdulaziz ibn Saud]] conquered the Hejaz and ousted the ruling [[Hashemite]] clan. The new [[Najd]]i rulers found themselves at the reins of a highly sophisticated society. A cohesive political structure based on the Majlis al-Shura (consultative council) system had been in place for centuries. A central administrative body managed an annual budget which allocated expenditure to secondary schools, military and police forces.<ref>{{cite book | last =  Yamani | first = Mai | title = Cradle of Islam | year = 2009 | publisher = I.B. TAURIS | location = London | isbn = 978-1-84511-824-2 | page = 2 | chapter = Devotion }}</ref>

Similarly, the religious fabric of the Najd and the Hejaz were vastly different. Traditional Hejazi cultural customs and rituals were almost entirely religious in nature. Celebrations honouring Muhammad, his [[Ahl al-Bayt|family]] and [[Companions of the Prophet|companions]], reverence of deceased saints, visitation of shrines, tombs and holy sites connected with any of these were among the customs indigenous to Hejazi Islam.<ref>{{cite book | last =  Yamani | first = Mai | title = Cradle of Islam | year = 2009 | publisher = I.B. TAURIS | location = London | isbn = 978-1-84511-824-2 | page = 4 | chapter = Devotion }}</ref> As administrative authority of the Hejaz passed into the hands of Najdi [[Wahhabism|Wahhabi]] Muslims from the interior, the Wahhabi [[ulama]] viewed local religious practices as unfounded superstition superseding codified religious sanction that was considered a total corruption of religion and the spreading of heresy.<ref>{{cite book | last =  Rentz | first = George S. | title = The Birth of the Islamic Reform Movement in Saudi Arabia | year = 2004 | publisher = Arabian Publishing Ltd. | location = London | isbn = 0-9544792-2-X | page = 139 | chapter = Devotion }}</ref> What followed was a removal of the physical infrastructure, tombs, mausoleums, mosques and sites associated with the family and companions of Muhammad.<ref>{{cite web| url = https://www.pbs.org/newshour/bb/middle_east/jan-june02/saudi_2-19.html| title = A NewsHour with Jim Lehrer Transcript| access-date = October 29, 2010| last = Angawi| first = Dr.Sami| date = February 19, 2002| publisher = PBS NewsHour Online Transcript| archive-date = October 24, 2010| archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20101024204249/http://www.pbs.org/newshour/bb/middle_east/jan-june02/saudi_2-19.html| url-status = dead}}</ref>

=== 19th century ===
{{See also|Demolition of al-Baqi|Wahhabi sack of Karbala}}
In 1801 and 1802, the Saudis under [[Abdul Aziz ibn Muhammad ibn Saud]] attacked and captured the [[Shia]] holy cities of [[Karbala]] and [[Najaf]] in today's [[Iraq]], massacred parts of the Shia Muslim population and destroyed the tomb of [[Husayn ibn Ali]], the grandson of Muhammad and son of [[Ali]], Muhammad's son-in-law. In 1803 and 1804, the Saudis captured Mecca and Medina and destroyed historical monuments and various holy Muslim sites and shrines, such as the shrine built over the tomb of [[Fatimah]], the daughter of Muhammad, and even intended to destroy the grave of Muhammad himself as idolatrous, causing outrage throughout the [[Muslim world]].<ref name="islamicamagazine.com"/><ref name="A Paladin Gears Up for War">Nibras Kazimi, [http://www.nysun.com/article/65662?page_no=3 A Paladin Gears Up for War] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080304153101/http://www.nysun.com/article/65662?page_no=3 |date=2008-03-04 }}, [[The New York Sun]], November 1, 2007</ref><ref name="Saudi's Shi'ites walk tightrope">John R Bradley, [https://web.archive.org/web/20050405031346/http://www.atimes.com/atimes/Middle_East/GC17Ak01.html Saudi's Shi'ites walk tightrope], ''[[Asia Times]]'', March 17, 2005</ref>

In Mecca, the tombs of direct relations of Muhammad located at [[Jannatul Mualla]] cemetery, including that of his first wife [[Khadijah bint Khuwaylid]], were demolished.<ref>{{cite web |last=Shehabi |first=Saeed |date=2008-02-08 |title=Destruction of Islamic Architectural Heritage in Saudi Arabia: A Wake-up Call |url=http://theamericanmuslim.org/tam.php/features/articles/destruction_of_islamic_architectural_heritage_in_saudi_arabia_a_wake_up_cal |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170316140422/http://theamericanmuslim.org/tam.php/features/articles/destruction_of_islamic_architectural_heritage_in_saudi_arabia_a_wake_up_cal |archive-date=16 March 2017 |access-date=14 November 2014 |website=The American Muslim (TAM) |publisher=Theamericanmuslim.org}}</ref> The initial dismantling of the sites began in 1806 when the Wahhabi army of the [[First Saudi State]] occupied Medina and systematically levelled many of the structures at the vast [[Jannat al-Baqi]] cemetery<ref>{{cite web|url=http://countrystudies.us/saudi-arabia/7.htm|title=The Saud Family and Wahhabi Islam|publisher=Countrystudies.us|access-date=14 November 2014|archive-date=16 March 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170316131703/http://countrystudies.us/saudi-arabia/7.htm|url-status=live}}</ref> adjacent to the Prophet's Mosque (Al-Masjid al-Nabawi) housing the remains of many of the members of Muhammad's family, close companions and central figures of early Islam. The [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Turks]], practitioners themselves of more tolerant and at times mystical strains of Islam, had erected elaborate mausoleums over the graves of Al-Baqi. These were levelled in their entirety. Mosques across the city were also targeted and an attempt was made to demolish Muhammad's tomb.<ref>{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tjpExwQWtOsC&pg=PA206|title=Saudi Arabia enters the 21st century|author=Anthony H. Cordesman|year=2003|quote=The tension between Saudi Shi'ite and Wahhabi is especially intense because Saudi "Wahhabis" actively reject all veneration of man, even the prophet.  At one point, they attempted to destroy Muhammad's tomb in Medina. In contrast, the Saudi Shi'ites are "[[Twelver]]s", a branch of Islam that venerates the Prophet's son-in-law Ali, and believes that the leadership of Islam must pass through Ali's line. They venerate each of the past imams, and make pilgrimages to their tombs.|publisher=Praeger (April 21, 2003)|isbn=978-0-275-98091-7|access-date=August 12, 2015|archive-date=January 18, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230118190440/https://books.google.com/books?id=tjpExwQWtOsC&pg=PA206|url-status=live}}</ref> Widespread vocal criticism of this last action by Muslim communities as far away as India, eventually led to abandoning any attempt on this site. Political claims made against Turkish control of the region initiated the [[Ottoman–Saudi war]] (1811–1818) in which the Saudi defeat forced Wahhabi tribesmen to retreat from the Hejaz back into the interior. Turkish forces reasserted control of the region and subsequently began extensive rebuilding of sacred sites between 1848 and 1860, with many of them employing the finest examples of Ottoman design and craftsmanship.<ref name="islamicamagazine.com">{{citation |first1=Irfan |last1=Ahmed |url=http://islamicamagazine.com/?p=424 |title=The Destruction of Holy Sites in Mecca and Medina |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110713063137/http://islamicamagazine.com/?p=424 |archive-date=13 July 2011 |work=[[Islamica Magazine]] |issue=15 |page=71 |access-date=29 October 2010 }} [http://www.think-israel.org/ahmed.shrinedestruction.html alternative url]</ref>

On 21 April 1925, the mausoleums and domes at [[Al-Baqi]] in Medina were once again levelled<ref name="islamicamagazine.com" /> and so were indicators of the exact location of the resting places of Muhammad's family members and descendants, as it remains to the present day. Portions of the famed [[Al-Burda|Qasida al-Burda]], the 13th-century ode written in praise of Muhammad by Imam [[al-Busiri]], inscribed over Muhammad's tomb, were painted over. Among specific sites targeted at this time were the graves of the Martyrs of the [[Battle of Uhud]], including the grave of the renowned [[Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib]], uncle of Muhammad and one of his most beloved supporters, the Mosque of Fatimah Al Zahraa', daughter of Muhammad, the Mosque of the Two Lighthouses (Manaratayn) as well as the Qubbat Al-Thanaya,<ref name="islamicamagazine.com" /> the cupola built as the burial place of Mohammad's incisor tooth, which was broken from a blow received during the Battle of Uhud. In Medina, the Mashrubat Umm Ibrahim, the home of Mohammad's Egyptian wife [[Maria al-Qibtiyya|Mariah]] and birthplace of their son [[Ibrahim ibn Muhammad|Ibrahim]], as well as the adjacent burial site of Hamida al-Barbariyya, mother of [[Imam Musa al-Kadhim|Musa al-Kadhim]], were destroyed during this time.<ref name="islamicamagazine.com" /> The site was paved over and is today part of the massive marble [[esplanade]] beside the mosque. The government-appointed permanent scholarly committee of Saudi Arabia has ordered the demolition of such structures in a series of Islamic rulings noting excessive veneration leading to [[Shirk (Islam)|shirk]] (idolatry).<ref name="Fatwas of the Permanent Committee">{{cite web | url=http://aliftaweb.org/English/Pages/FatwasPermanentCommittee.aspx?cultStr=en&View=Tree&NodeID=8510&PageNo=1&BookID=7 | title=Fatwas of the Permanent Committee | publisher=Official KSA Rulings | access-date=27 March 2014 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141213042446/http://aliftaweb.org/English/Pages/FatwasPermanentCommittee.aspx?cultStr=en&View=Tree&NodeID=8510&PageNo=1&BookID=7 | archive-date=13 December 2014 | url-status=dead }}</ref>

=== 21st century ===
The twenty-first century has seen an increase in the demolition of sites in Mecca and Medina by Saudi authorities, alongside the expansion of luxury development.

As the annual ''[[hajj]]'' continues to draw larger crowds year after year, the Saudi authorities deemed it necessary to raze large tracts of formerly residential areas around the two important mosques to make way for pilgrimage-related [[infrastructure]]. In 2010, it was forecast that developers were going to spend an estimated $13 billion on the largest expansion project in the city's history.<ref>{{cite web| url = http://www.islamicpluralism.org/467/dr-sami-angawi-on-wahhabi-desecration-of-makkah| title = Dr.Sami Angawi on Wahhabi Desecration of Makkah| access-date = November 28, 2010| last = Abou-Ragheb| first = Laith| date = July 12, 2005| publisher = Center for Islamic Pluralism| archive-date = July 22, 2016| archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20160722073326/http://www.islamicpluralism.org/467/dr-sami-angawi-on-wahhabi-desecration-of-makkah| url-status = live}}</ref>

While there is widespread agreement for the need of facilities that can accommodate greater numbers of pilgrims, the development of upscale hotels and condominium towers, restaurants, shopping centres and spas<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.fairmont.com/makkah|title=Makkah Hotels: Makkah Hotel at Fairmont|publisher=Fairmont.com|access-date=8 December 2014|archive-date=5 February 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170205210918/http://www.fairmont.com/makkah/|url-status=live}}</ref> has caused some to criticize the over-[[commercialization]] of a site which many consider to be a divinely ordained [[sanctuary]] for Muslims.

The rapid influx of capital investment in Mecca and Medina leads many to believe that money and [[economic growth]] are the ultimate reason for Saudi authorities. Critics argue that this monetary focus works with [[Wahhabi]] state [[policy]] that imposes a massive cultural and social deletion within the Holy Cities,<ref>{{cite web| url = http://www.torontosun.com/travel/international/2010/11/12/16107661-reuters.html| title = Mecca goes Upmarket| access-date = December 1, 2010| last = Laessing| first = Ulf| date = November 18, 2010| publisher = Reuters| archive-date = November 21, 2010| archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20101121045456/http://www.torontosun.com/travel/international/2010/11/12/16107661-reuters.html| url-status = live}}</ref> erasing any elements that encourage practices counter to the Wahhabi creed.

According to ''[[The Independent]]'', the House of Mawalid where Muhammad is said to have been born is about to be replaced by a huge royal palace, as a part of a multibillion-pound construction project in Mecca which has resulted in the destruction of hundreds of historic monuments.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/mecca-under-threat-outrage-at-plan-to-destroy-the-birthplace-of-the-prophet-mohamed-and-replace-it-with-a-new-palace-and-luxury-malls-9857098.html|title=Mecca under threat: Outrage at plan to destroy the 'birthplace' of the Prophet Mohamed and replace it with a new palace and luxury malls|work=The Independent|access-date=14 November 2014|archive-date=13 November 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141113045400/http://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/mecca-under-threat-outrage-at-plan-to-destroy-the-birthplace-of-the-prophet-mohamed-and-replace-it-with-a-new-palace-and-luxury-malls-9857098.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

The Saudis are turning [[Diriyah]], the demolished capital of the [[Emirate of Diriyah|First Saudi State]], into a major tourist attraction.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2015/06/01/world/middleeast/saudis-turn-birthplace-of-ideology-into-tourist-spot.html|title=Saudis Turn Birthplace of Wahhabism Ideology Into Tourist Spot|first=Ben|last=Hubbard|date=31 May 2015|work=[[The New York Times]]}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.arabnews.com/node/1034231/saudi-arabia  |title=Diriyah on course to become world-class tourist spot |author=Estimo Jr, Rodolfo|date=5 January 2017|work=[[Arab News]]|access-date=21 July 2017}}</ref>

=== Historical religious sites ===
* ''Bayt al-Mawlid'' ("House of the [[Mawlid|Birth]]"), where Muhammad is believed to have been born in 570. Originally turned into a [[Makkah Al Mukarramah Library|library]], it now lies under a rundown building which was built 70 years ago as a compromise after Wahhabi clerics called for it to be demolished.<ref name=LAT>Salah Nasrawi,[https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2007-sep-16-adfg-mecca16-story.html "Mecca's ancient heritage is under attack – Developments for pilgrims and the strict beliefs of Saudi clerics are encroaching on or eliminating Islam's holy sites in the kingdom"], ''[[Los Angeles Times]]'', September 16, 2007. Accessed online 16 December 2008.</ref>
* The house of Khadija, Muhammad's first wife. Muslims believe he received some of the first revelations there. It was also where his children [[Zainab bint Muhammad]], [[Ruqayyah bint Muhammad]], [[Umm Kulthum bint Muhammad]], [[Fatimah]], [[Qasim ibn Muhammad|Qasim]] and [[Abd-Allah ibn Muhammad]] were born. After it was rediscovered during the Haram extensions in 1989, it was covered over and it was made into a library.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Tariq |first=Arsalan |date=2026-02-09 |title=House of Khadija (RA) {{!}} Everything You Need To Know {{!}} |url=https://thepilgrim.co/house-of-khadija-ra/ |access-date=2026-10-09 |website=The Pilgrim |language=en-US}}</ref>

''[[Time (magazine)|Time]]''.</ref>
* House of Muhammed in Medina, where he lived after the migration from Mecca.<ref name=AL />
* ''Dar Al-Arqam'', the first Islamic school where Muhammad taught.<ref name=LAT /> It now lies under the extension of the [[Great Mosque of Mecca|Masjid Al-Haram of Mecca]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Dar al-Arqam: The First Secret Centre of Islamic Preaching |url=https://hisarbalkans.com/en/page/1398/dar-al-arqam/ |access-date=2026-10-09 |website=Hisar Balkans |language=en}}</ref>
* ''Qubbat al-Thanaya'', the burial site of Muhammed's [[incisor]] that was broken in the [[Battle of Uhud]].<ref name="islamicamagazine.com" />
* ''Mashrabat Umm Ibrahim'', built to mark the location of the house where Muhammad's son, [[Ibrahim ibn Muhammad|Ibrahim]], was born to [[Maria al-Qibtiyya|Mariah]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Mashrabat Umm Ibrahim Mosque: Historical Site in Medina |url=https://ziyarago.com/en/places/place/mosque/masjid-mashrabat-umm-ibrahim |access-date=2026-10-09 |website=ZiyaraGo |language=en}}</ref>
