The '''Djinguereber Mosque''' ({{langx|ar|مسجد دجينجيربر}}; [[French language|French]]: ''Mosquée de Djinguereber''; from [[Koyra Chiini language|Koyra Chiini]] ''jiŋgar-ey beer'' 'grand mosque'<ref>{{cite book |last1=Heath |first1=Jeffrey |title=Koyra Chiini. (Dictionnaire songhay-anglais-français, I.) |date=1998 |publisher=L'Harmattan |location=Paris |isbn=2-7384-6726-1 |page=146}}</ref>), also known as '''Djingareyber''' or '''Djingarey Ber''', is a famous learning center in [[Timbuktu]], [[Mali]]. Built in 1327, it is one of three mosques associated with the historical community of scholars and students known today as the "[[University of Timbuktu]]". It was inscribed on the list of [[UNESCO World Heritage Site]]s in 1988.<ref name="UNESCO1988">{{Citation| title=Report of the World Heritage Committee, Twelfth Session | pages=17–18 | publisher=UNESCO Convention Concerning the Protection of World Cultural and Natural Heritage | date=1988-12-23 | location=Brasilia <!--, Brazil, 5–9 December 1988 -->| url=https://whc.unesco.org/archive/repcom88.htm#119 | id=SC-88/CONF.001/13 | access-date=2007-04-09 }}</ref>

==Design==
The design and construction of the Djinguereber mosque is traditionally credited to the [[Al-Andalus|Andalusi]] scholar [[al-Sahili|Abu Ishaq Al Sahili]]. According to [[Ibn Khaldun]], one of the best-known sources on 14th-century [[Mali Empire|Mali]], he was said to have received 12,000 [[mithkal]]s of gold dust for the work. More recent analyses reject this version, demonstrating that the architectural style of the Djinguereber Mosque and others in West Africa derives mainly from mosques in the Sahara and traditional African architecture and religions, so that al-Sahili's influence on West African architecture is treated as a myth.<ref>The Meanings of Timbuktu, Bloom, Pg. 52.</ref><ref name="Aradeon, Susan B. 1989 1-2">{{cite journal|author=Aradeon, Susan B.|title=Al-Sahili: the historians' myth of architectural technology transfer from North Africa |journal=Journal des africanistes|date=1989|volume=59|issue=1–2|pages=99–131|doi=10.3406/jafr.1989.2279 }}</ref>

Except for a small part of the northern facade, which was reinforced in the 1960s in alhore ([[limestone block]]s, also widely used in the rest of the town), and the minaret, also built in limestone and rendered with mud,<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.archnet.org/sites/4668 |title=Djingereyber Mosque Restoration |website=Archnet.org}}</ref> the Djingareyber Mosque is made entirely of earth plus organic materials such as fibre, straw and wood. It has three inner courts, two minarets and twenty-five rows of pillars aligned in an east-west direction and a prayer space for 2,000 people.

==History==
===Foundation of the mosque===
The creation of Djinguereber mosque is attributed to [[Mansa Musa]] in 1325. Its [[Sudano-Sahelian architecture]] differs stylistically from the mosques of North Africa and Andalusia.<ref name="Aradeon, Susan B. 1989 1-2" /> 
The mosque is sometimes credited to the Granadan poet [[al-Sahili|Abū Isḥāq al-Sāḥilī]], whom Mansa Musa brought to Mali after the 1324 hajj, but [[John Hunwick|J. O. Hunwick]] has shown that al-Sāḥilī is firmly attested in the Arabic sources only as the designer of a royal audience chamber at the city of Mali, and that his role there appears to have been organisational rather than that of a structural architect.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Hunwick |first=J. O. |title=An Andalusian in Mali: a contribution to the biography of Abū Ishāq al-Sāhilī, c. 1290–1346 |journal=Paideuma |volume=36 |year=1990 |pages=59–66 |jstor=40732660}}</ref>

===Medieval period===
During the reign of [[Askia Dawud]] of the [[Songhai Empire]], Djinguereber mosque was renovated by the Qadi of Timbuktu [[Al-Qadi Aqib ibn Mahmud ibn Umar|Aqib ibn Mahmud]] beginning in 1570. The work was a source of conflict between the Askia and the Qadi, who resented the renovated mosque's association with a secular power.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Gomez |first1=Michael |title=African dominion : a new history of empire in early and medieval West Africa |date=2018 |publisher=Princeton University Press |location=Princeton, NJ |page=352 |isbn=9780691177427}}</ref>

===Post-independence===

On 26 February 2010, during [[Mawlid]] (a festival to mark the birth anniversary of [[Muhammad]]), a stampede at the mosque killed around 26 people and injured at least 55 others, mostly women and children.<ref>{{citation |date=26 Feb 2010 | url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/africa/8538312.stm | title=Deadly crush at Timbuktu mosque | publisher=BBC News | access-date=14 Jun 2010}}; {{citation | date=26 Feb 2010 | url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/592/ | title=Pilgrims killed in stampede at Djingareyber Mosque in Timbuktu | publisher=UNESCO World Heritage News Archive |access-date=14 Jun 2010  }}; {{citation | date=26 Feb 2010 | url=https://www.reuters.com/article/idUSTRE61P37K20100226 | title=26 dead in Timbuktu mosque stampede | publisher=Reuters | access-date=14 Jun 2010 }}; [[n:At least fifteen dead after stampede at Mali mosque|Wikinews]]</ref>

=== Attack in 2012 ===
{{Further|Islamist destruction of Timbuktu heritage sites}}
On 1 July 2012, militant Islamists of the [[Ansar Dine]] ("defenders of faith") began destroying the tombs of Timbuktu shortly after UNESCO placed them on a list of endangered World Heritage sites. They set about destroying seven of Timbuktu's total sixteen ancient Muslim saint shrines,<ref name="rnw.nl"/> including two tombs at the Djingareyber Mosque.<ref name="rnw.nl">[http://www.rnw.nl/africa/bulletin/mali-islamists-destroy-tombs-ancient-timbuktu-mosque Mali Islamists destroy tombs at ancient Timbuktu mosque {{pipe}} Radio Netherlands Worldwide] {{webarchive |url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131029193235/http://www.rnw.nl/africa/bulletin/mali-islamists-destroy-tombs-ancient-timbuktu-mosque |date=29 October 2013 }}</ref> Using "hoes, pick-axes and chisels, they hammered away at the two earthen tombs until they were completely destroyed".<ref name="rnw.nl"/>  The damage to the mosque itself, however, was minimal.<ref>{{cite web |title=Timbuktu's Djinguereber mosque: a history of cities in 50 buildings, day 5 |url=https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2015/mar/27/timbuktu-djinguereber-mosque-history-cities-buildings |website=The Guardian|date=27 March 2015 |access-date=11 September 2015}}</ref>

== Preservation efforts ==
The mosque was on the UN's list of World Heritage Sites in danger in 1990 due to sand encroachment from increasing [[desertification]].<ref name="UNESCO1990">{{Citation| title = Report of the World Heritage Committee, Fourteenth Session | pages = 17–18 | publisher = UNESCO Convention Concerning the Protection of World Cultural and Natural Heritage | date = 1990-12-12 | location = Banff<!--, Alberta, Canada, 7–12 December 1990--> | url = https://whc.unesco.org/archive/repcom90.htm#timbuktu | id = CLT-90/CONF.004/13 | access-date = 2007-04-09 }}</ref> A four-year project towards the restoration and rehabilitation of the Mosque began in June 2006, and is being conducted and financed by the [[Aga Khan Trust for Culture]].<ref name="Afribone20070405">{{cite news |url=http://www.afribone.com/article.php3?id_article=6085 |title=La Cité des 333 saints abrite de nombreux chantiers de modernisation |publisher=Afribone Mali SA |language=French |date=2007-04-05 |access-date=2007-04-09}}</ref>

The first phase of restoration was a pilot project undertaken from November 2006 to July 2007. This work included drainage and paving around the mosque, re-rendering walls in bad condition and in one zone of the roof, replacing around 50% of the beams, above which was a heavy build-up of mud plaster. The local masons in charge of the project clearly had good technical expertise; however, there is a need to regularly document their activities and starting point.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/soc/928|title=UNESCO World Heritage Centre - State of Conservation (SOC 2008) Timbuktu (Mali) |author=UNESCO World Heritage Centre |website=whc.unesco.org |language=en|access-date=2018-10-08}}</ref> Natural local trees that were originally used for building materials for the beams in the mosque have also disappeared due to climate change, so wood beams must be imported from [[Ghana]]. This drastically increases the price of resources needed to restore the mosque, as building materials are not readily available.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2015/mar/27/timbuktu-djinguereber-mosque-history-cities-buildings |title=Timbuktu's Djinguereber mosque: a history of cities in 50 buildings, day 5 |last=Smith|first=Alex Duval |date=2015-03-27 |website=the Guardian |language=en |access-date=2018-10-08}}</ref> Despite ongoing maintenance efforts, there is still a one-meter difference between the roof height in 1952 and today.{{citation needed|date=May 2024}}

While drought may cause issues, too much rain has also shown to be detrimental to the mosque. Heavy rains in 1999, 2001 and 2003 caused the collapse of many traditionally built earthen buildings, as well as more recently built structures. Climate change is expected to increase the severity of these threats.<ref>Colette, Augustin, and Kishore Rao. ''Case Studies on Climate Change and World Heritage''. UNESCO World Heritage Centre, 2009, pp. 74–75, ''Case Studies on Climate Change and World Heritage''.</ref>

After being de-listed in 2005, the Djinguereber has once again been on the list of World Heritage Sites in danger since the attack by Ansar Dine in 2012.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/soc/4286|title=State of Conservation Timbuktu (2023) |author=UNESCO World Heritage Centre |website=whc.unesco.org |language=en|access-date=2024-05-05}}</ref>
